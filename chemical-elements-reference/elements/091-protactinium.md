# Element 91: Protactinium

**Symbol:** Pa  
**Atomic number:** 91  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 231.035 88(1)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f² 6d 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁴K₁₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 5.89 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring nuclide:** ²³¹Pa  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear, radiochemical and material-property data require additional evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Protactinium is an actinoid with neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f²6d7s² and ground term ⁴K₁₁/₂. Its common oxidation states are +4 and +5, with +5 especially important in aqueous and oxo-chemistry. Pa(V) forms strongly bound oxo-species and complexes that can exhibit hydrolysis and adsorption behaviour distinct from uranium and thorium. The 5f electrons are less shielded than lanthanoid 4f electrons and contribute to more varied bonding and oxidation-state chemistry.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear properties
CIAAW gives 231.03588(1) as the standard atomic weight, determined principally from ²³¹Pa, the only naturally occurring isotope listed in the 2024 isotopic-composition table. ²³¹Pa has a half-life of approximately 32,760 years and occurs in the uranium-235 decay series. ²³⁴Pa is a short-lived decay product in the uranium-238 series. Protactinium is not primordial in the practical geochemical sense: terrestrial quantities are maintained by radioactive decay of uranium-bearing minerals. Nuclear level data and decay branches must be taken from current ENSDF/NuDat evaluations.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
Protactinium is a dense, silvery radioactive metal. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1572 °C, boiling point 4000 °C and density 15.4 g cm⁻³. These are compiled reference values; experimental bulk properties are difficult to establish because of scarcity, radiotoxicity and self-heating. Physical datasets should record sample isotope, purity, phase, temperature and measurement uncertainty.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Protactinium is commonly +5 in oxidising aqueous systems and +4 under more reducing conditions. Pa(V) forms oxo-species and hydrolyses strongly; adsorption onto mineral surfaces and colloids can control its mobility in natural waters and sediments. Its separation from uranium and thorium is complicated by related actinoid chemistry and the need to control oxidation state and complexation. Speciation claims should identify pH, redox potential, ligand and ionic strength.

## 5. Research and applications
Protactinium has few practical applications outside specialised nuclear chemistry and geochemical research. The ²³¹Pa isotope is used in uranium-series and oceanographic studies, including investigations of sediment processes and past ocean circulation. Its isotope ratios can provide information on particle scavenging and water-mass transport, but interpretation requires coupled modelling and careful sample preparation.

## 6. Radiological safety
Protactinium is radioactive and chemically toxic. Its long-lived alpha-emitting isotopes present internal contamination hazards, particularly through inhalation or ingestion of dust and soluble compounds. Handling requires licensed radiochemical facilities, contamination control, appropriate containment and isotope-specific dosimetry. Bulk metal assumptions are not appropriate for trace radiochemical sources.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Protactinium was identified independently by Kasimir Fajans and Oswald Göhring in 1913 and later isolated and characterised by Otto Hahn and Lise Meitner. Its name derives from Greek *protos* and *actinium*, referring to its position in the actinium decay chain as the parent of actinium-227.
