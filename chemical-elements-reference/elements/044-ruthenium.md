# Element 44: Ruthenium

**Symbol:** Ru  
**Atomic number:** 44  
**Group:** 8  
**Period:** 5  
**Block:** d-block  
**Standard atomic weight:** 101.07(2)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 4d⁷ 5s¹  
**Ground-state term:** ⁵F₅  
**First ionization energy:** 7.3605 eV  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic structure and quantum properties
Ruthenium is a period-5 d-block element. Its tabulated ground-state electron configuration is [Kr] 4d⁷ 5s¹. The ground-state term and first ionization energy are taken from the NIST-reviewed periodic-table compilation. These summary values do not replace a level-resolved treatment: a research-grade spectral appendix should query NIST ASD for level energies, J values, transition probabilities, wavelengths, isotope shifts, and uncertainties.

## 3. Physical properties and phase behaviour
- **Melting point:** 2606 K (2333 °C)
- **Boiling point:** 4420 K (4147 °C)
- **Density:** 12.1 g cm⁻³
- **Ambient crystal structure:** hcp, space group P6₃/mmc under ambient conditions

These are reference values, not a critical thermodynamic compilation. Density and transition temperatures depend on purity, porosity, isotopic composition where relevant, pressure, and measurement convention. The hcp/fcc assignments describe the ambient elemental metal, not all compounds or metastable thin-film phases.

## 4. Chemical thermodynamics, oxidation states, and coordination chemistry
Ruthenium chemistry spans multiple oxidation states, notably +2, +3, +4, and +8 in selected compounds. RuO₄ is a volatile, powerful oxidant and must not be treated as representative of the much less volatile metal or common ruthenium salts. Ru(II)/Ru(III) complexes display rich ligand-field, redox, photophysical, and catalytic chemistry. Ruthenium complexes are widely studied in homogeneous catalysis, photochemistry, and medicinal inorganic chemistry.

A final thermodynamic dataset should provide standard-state formation enthalpies, Gibbs energies, entropies, heat capacities, redox potentials, aqueous speciation constants, and temperature/pH dependence with traceable primary or critically evaluated sources. No unverified numerical thermodynamic constants are inserted in this draft.

## 5. Materials science and catalytic function
Applications include electrical contacts and thick-film resistors, dimensionally stable anodes based on RuO₂ coatings, catalysts for hydrogenation and oxidation, ammonia-related catalysis, and specialized hard alloys. Ruthenium-based molecular complexes are research platforms for photoredox catalysis and bioinorganic studies. Thin-film and oxide properties depend strongly on phase, stoichiometry, substrate, and deposition conditions.

For engineering use, catalytic activity must be reported with reaction conditions, support composition, loading, particle-size distribution, space velocity, feed composition, conversion, selectivity, and deactivation history. Bulk elemental properties alone cannot predict supported-catalyst performance.

## 6. Toxicology, radiological safety, and handling
Finely divided ruthenium may present dust and fire concerns. RuO₄ is acutely hazardous because it is volatile, strongly oxidizing, and highly toxic/corrosive; rigorous containment is required. Toxicological conclusions must be species-specific and should not be generalized from Ru metal to RuO₄ or organoruthenium complexes.
