# Element 88: Radium

**Symbol:** Ra  
**Atomic number:** 88  
**Group / period / block:** 2 / 7 / s  
**Standard atomic weight:** none (no stable isotopes); representative mass number [226]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 5.278423 eV (NIST)  
**Important isotopes:** ²²³Ra, ²²⁴Ra, ²²⁶Ra and ²²⁸Ra  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific dosimetry and radiochemical data require rigorous evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Radium is the heaviest alkaline-earth element. Its neutral ground configuration is [Rn]7s² and its ground term is ¹S₀. The dominant oxidation state is +2, yielding Ra²⁺. Radium chemistry resembles barium chemistry in many respects, but radioactive decay and radiological containment dominate its practical handling. Relativistic effects influence its atomic structure and bonding.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Radium has no stable isotopes and therefore no standard atomic weight. The bracketed mass number [226] is a representative value. Important isotopes include ²²³Ra (half-life about 11.43 days), ²²⁴Ra (about 3.66 days), ²²⁶Ra (about 1600 years) and ²²⁸Ra (about 5.76 years). ²²⁶Ra is a member of the ²³⁸U decay series and produces ²²²Rn among its decay progeny. ²²³Ra is an alpha emitter used in a regulated radiopharmaceutical for selected cases of metastatic prostate cancer involving bone. Nuclear decay schemes and emissions must be quoted from evaluated ENSDF/NuDat data.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar radium is a soft, silvery radioactive solid. The RSC compilation lists melting point 696 °C, boiling point 1500 °C and density about 5 g cm⁻³. The values are compiled reference figures; direct physical measurements are limited by the element's radioactivity and scarcity. Radium metal oxidises in air and reacts with water, so surface condition and containment influence measurements.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Radium occurs almost exclusively in the +2 oxidation state. Ra²⁺ forms salts analogous to other alkaline-earth cations, including chloride, carbonate and sulfate compounds. Radium sulfate is poorly soluble, a property relevant to environmental geochemistry and historical radium separation. Its ionic size and chemical similarity to calcium and barium influence biological distribution and mineral uptake.

## 5. Applications and history of use
Radium was historically used in luminous paints, radiotherapy sources and research, but most such uses were discontinued because of radiation hazards. Radium-223 dichloride is used in a specialised medical setting because calcium-like chemistry promotes association with areas of increased bone turnover; the alpha-emitting decay chain can irradiate nearby tumour cells. This is a regulated medicinal use and requires isotope-specific clinical protocols and dosimetry, not general handling of radium metal.

## 6. Radiological safety and toxicology
Radium is a serious radiological hazard. Because Ra²⁺ behaves chemically in some ways like calcium, ingested radium can be deposited in bone, where alpha-emitting decay products deliver sustained internal radiation. Inhalation or ingestion of radium-containing dusts and salts requires strict prevention. The decay chain can generate radon gas and other radioactive progeny. Work with radium requires specialised containment, contamination monitoring, licensed waste management and isotope-specific dose assessment.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Radium was discovered in 1898 by Marie and Pierre Curie during the study of uranium ores. Its name derives from Latin *radius*, meaning ray, reflecting its radioactivity. The discovery helped establish the science of radioactivity and led to major changes in nuclear physics, medicine and radiation protection.
