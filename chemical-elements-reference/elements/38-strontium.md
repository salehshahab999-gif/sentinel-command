# Element 38: Strontium

**Symbol:** Sr  
**Atomic number:** 38  
**Group:** 2  
**Period:** 5  
**Block:** s-block  
**Classification:** Alkaline-earth metal  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** 87.62(1)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 5s²  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Strontium is a period-5 s-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Kr] 5s². The periodic classification is useful for orientation, but chemical properties must be interpreted in terms of electronic structure, phase, oxidation state, and reaction conditions.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Natural strontium has four stable isotopes: ⁸⁴Sr, ⁸⁶Sr, ⁸⁷Sr, and ⁸⁸Sr. CIAAW representative amount fractions are 0.0056(2), 0.0986(20), 0.0700(20), and 0.8258(35). The ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr ratio varies naturally because radiogenic ⁸⁷Sr accumulates through beta-minus decay of ⁸⁷Rb. This variability is why CIAAW retains the standard atomic weight 87.62(1) and why Rb–Sr dating is geochemically useful. Artificial fission product ⁹⁰Sr is radioactive and must be treated separately from natural stable isotopes.

## 3. Physical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar strontium is a soft, silvery alkaline-earth metal. RSC reference values are melting point 1050 K, boiling point 1650 K, and density 2.64 g cm⁻³. Fresh metal tarnishes and reacts with air and water; measured surface properties depend on oxidation state and sample handling.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Strontium commonly forms Sr²⁺ and ionic compounds. Its chemistry resembles calcium and barium, with important carbonate, sulfate, oxide, and halide compounds. Strontium carbonate and celestite (SrSO₄) are important minerals. The similarity of Sr²⁺ to Ca²⁺ also explains its incorporation into biological mineral phases and bone.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Strontium salts produce intense red colours in flares and fireworks. Strontium ferrites are used in permanent magnets, while strontium aluminate phosphors provide persistent afterglow materials. Radiostrontium isotopes have specialized uses in sealed sources and historical radioisotope power systems, but ⁹⁰Sr use entails strict radiological controls.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Stable strontium compounds have compound-specific hazards and should not be treated as equivalent to radioactive strontium. ⁹⁰Sr is a high-concern beta-emitting fission product; because strontium behaves chemically like calcium, it can enter bone and irradiate bone tissue and marrow. Environmental and occupational assessments must distinguish isotope, chemical form, and exposure route.
