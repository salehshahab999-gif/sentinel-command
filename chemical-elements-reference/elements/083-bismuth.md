# Element 83: Bismuth

**Symbol:** Bi  
**Atomic number:** 83  
**Group / period / block:** 15 / 6 / p  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 208.980 40(1)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s² 6p³  
**Ground-state term:** ⁴S°₃/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 7.2855 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring nuclide:** ²⁰⁹Bi (monoisotopic)  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear-decay and compound-specific toxicology data require source-level verification.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Bismuth is a heavy pnictogen with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s²6p³ and ground term ⁴S°₃/₂. The +3 oxidation state is common, while +5 occurs in selected oxides and oxoanions. The inert-pair effect stabilises the 6s² pair and helps make Bi(III) more representative than Bi(V) in many chemical environments. Relativistic effects are important in the bonding and stereochemical behaviour of bismuth compounds.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear properties
Natural bismuth is effectively monoisotopic: ²⁰⁹Bi has representative amount fraction 1 and is the only naturally occurring isotope. CIAAW gives the standard atomic weight 208.98040(1). ²⁰⁹Bi is not strictly stable: it undergoes alpha decay with an extraordinarily long half-life, approximately 2.0×10¹⁹ years. Its decay is negligible over ordinary human and industrial timescales but relevant to modern nuclear physics. The exact evaluated half-life and decay energy should be taken from ENSDF/NuDat.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At ambient conditions bismuth is a brittle, silvery metal with a pinkish surface tint and a rhombohedral A7-type structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 271.406 °C, boiling point 1564 °C and density 9.79 g cm⁻³. Bismuth expands upon solidification, an unusual property among metals. Its low melting point and relatively low toxicity compared with lead have supported use in selected low-melting alloys, though compound-specific hazard assessment remains necessary.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Bismuth commonly forms +3 compounds including Bi₂O₃, BiCl₃ and bismuth(III) salts. Bi(V) compounds such as bismuthates are stronger oxidants and are less common. Bismuth(III) has a stereochemically active 6s² lone pair in many compounds, influencing coordination geometry and solid-state structures. Hydrolysis, complexation and precipitation depend on pH and ligand environment.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Bismuth is used in low-melting alloys, fusible safety devices, solders, free-machining alloys, specialised glass and selected thermoelectric materials. Bismuth subsalicylate is used in certain gastrointestinal medicines; this application belongs to a specific compound, not elemental metal. Bismuth-containing materials are also studied as lower-lead or lead-free alternatives in selected applications. Performance depends on alloy composition, phase distribution and microstructure.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Bismuth metal and many common bismuth compounds generally have lower toxicity than lead, but this does not make every bismuth compound risk-free. Solubility, anion, particle size and exposure route matter. Bismuth-containing pharmaceutical products have their own dose and use restrictions. Industrial dust and refining residues require normal metal-hygiene and waste controls.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Bismuth was known in the Middle Ages and was historically confused with lead and tin. Its symbol Bi comes from the modern element name, whose etymology is associated with the German *Wismut*. The recognition of bismuth as a distinct element developed through early modern metallurgy and chemical analysis.
