# Element 71: Lutetium

**Symbol:** Lu  
**Atomic number:** 71  
**Group / period / block:** 3 / 6 / d (lanthanoid family)  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 174.966 69(5), revised in 2024  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ²D₃/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 5.42586 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁷⁵Lu and ¹⁷⁶Lu  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear, geochronological and detailed materials data require additional audit.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Lutetium is the terminal lanthanoid and has the neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d6s². Its filled 4f shell and one 5d electron give the ²D₃/₂ ground term. The predominant oxidation state is +3, yielding Lu³⁺ with the closed-shell [Xe]4f¹⁴ configuration. This closed 4f shell makes Lu³⁺ diamagnetic and provides a useful endpoint for discussions of lanthanoid contraction. The placement of lutetium in group 3 and its classification as a lanthanoid reflect different periodic-table conventions; the configuration shows that the neutral atom's differentiating electron is in 5d.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW's 2024 representative isotopic composition is ¹⁷⁵Lu 0.97414(5) and ¹⁷⁶Lu 0.02586(5). The current standard atomic weight is 174.96669(5), revised in 2024 after new multi-collector ICP-MS measurements. ¹⁷⁶Lu is radioactive, with a half-life of 3.57(14)×10¹⁰ years, and decays predominantly by beta-minus emission to ¹⁷⁶Hf. This decay system is the basis of Lu-Hf geochronology. The isotope ratio is sensitive to geological evolution and mineral differentiation; interpretation requires decay constants, isotope-ratio measurements and a model for parent-daughter system closure.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar lutetium is a hard, dense, silvery metal with a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation lists a melting point of 1663 °C, boiling point of 3402 °C and density of 9.84 g cm⁻³. Its high density and relatively high melting point are consistent with strong metallic bonding. A precision reference should record temperature, pressure, purity and crystallographic state for density, thermal expansion and elastic properties.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Lutetium is overwhelmingly trivalent in ordinary inorganic chemistry. Lu₂O₃ is a stable sesquioxide, and Lu³⁺ forms high-coordinate complexes with hard oxygen- and nitrogen-donor ligands. Its small ionic radius, among the smallest for Ln³⁺, reflects the lanthanoid contraction. Lu compounds are often used as diamagnetic or weakly perturbing host lattices for optical and spectroscopic studies of other lanthanoid ions.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Lutetium has comparatively limited bulk industrial use because of its scarcity and separation cost. Applications include specialised catalysts, phosphors, scintillators and research materials. Lutetium oxyorthosilicate (LSO) and related lutetium-based scintillators are important in medical imaging detectors; performance depends on dopant concentration, crystal defects, optical coupling and detector geometry. Lu-177, a reactor-produced radionuclide, is used in targeted radionuclide therapy in radiopharmaceutical compounds; this medical use is isotope- and molecule-specific and is distinct from the chemistry of stable bulk lutetium.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Lutetium has no known biological role. Bulk metal and powders should be handled using standard rare-earth-metal precautions, while radioactive Lu-177 requires licensed radiopharmaceutical controls, activity measurement and isotope-specific dosimetry. Toxicity and environmental persistence depend on chemical form, solubility and exposure route. Extraction and separation wastes may contain multiple rare-earth elements and process reagents.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Lutetium was independently identified in the early twentieth century, with discovery credited to Georges Urbain in 1907. Its name derives from *Lutetia*, the Latin name for Paris. The final separation of lutetium from ytterbium-containing fractions marked the end of the classical discovery sequence of the naturally occurring lanthanoids.
