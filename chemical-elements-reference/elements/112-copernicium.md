# Element 112: Copernicium

**Symbol:** Cn  
**Atomic number:** 112  
**Group / period / block:** 12 / 7 / d  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [285]  
**Predicted ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 6d¹⁰ 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** theoretical/compiled values only; no high-precision direct measurement  
**Representative isotope:** ²⁸⁵Cn  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; atom-at-a-time adsorption data exist, but bulk physical and chemical properties remain unknown.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Copernicium is a transactinoid in group 12, below zinc, cadmium and mercury. Its predicted neutral ground configuration is [Rn]5f¹⁴6d¹⁰7s², with a closed-shell ¹S₀ ground term. Relativistic stabilisation of the 7s² pair is expected to influence its volatility and metallic character. It is often predicted to be unusually volatile for a metal, but this expectation is based on heavy-atom theory and atom-at-a-time experiments rather than bulk samples.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Copernicium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight. [285] is a representative mass number. CIAAW/NUBASE2020 list ²⁸⁵Cn with a half-life of 30(8) seconds. Other isotopes have different decay properties; each nuclear claim must be tied to the specific isotope and current ENSDF/NuDat evaluation.

## 3. Physical properties and measurement limits
No macroscopic sample of copernicium exists. RSC lists melting point, boiling point and density as unknown. It is labelled solid in standard periodic-table displays, but its actual bulk state at 20 °C cannot be experimentally determined. Predictions of liquid-like or highly volatile behaviour are theoretical and should not be presented as settled facts.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Copernicium is expected to have group-12 chemistry, but strong relativistic effects may make it more volatile and less metallic than mercury. Atom-at-a-time adsorption experiments on gold surfaces have been interpreted as evidence that Cn is highly volatile and interacts relatively weakly with gold, broadly consistent with a mercury-like or even more volatile metal. The number of atoms studied is very small, and the interpretation remains constrained by nuclear decay and surface-model uncertainties.

## 5. Research relevance
Copernicium has no commercial or technological use. Its importance is in testing relativistic chemistry, the stability of closed-shell heavy atoms and the periodic trends of group 12. Experimental studies use accelerator production, rapid gas transport and single-atom decay detection.

## 6. Radiological safety
Copernicium isotopes are radioactive and short-lived. Research is limited to specialised nuclear-research facilities with licensed controls, containment and isotope-specific monitoring. No bulk chemical handling or occupational exposure data exist.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Copernicium was first produced at GSI Darmstadt in 1996 by a collaboration led by Sigurd Hofmann. IUPAC recognised the discovery in 2009 and approved the name copernicium in 2010, in honour of astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus.

## 8. Evidence quality and unresolved work
- **Atomic structure:** NIST/PDG compilations provide the closed-shell ground-state assignment; ionisation and excited-state data require current theoretical/experimental review.
- **Nuclear properties:** CIAAW/NUBASE2020 lists ²⁸⁵Cn at 30(8) seconds; current ENSDF is required for decay details.
- **Physical properties:** RSC lists melting point, boiling point and density as unknown.
- **Pending:** repeatable adsorption measurements; quantified Cn-gold interaction energies; oxidation-state and compound formation studies; improved atomic and nuclear decay data.
