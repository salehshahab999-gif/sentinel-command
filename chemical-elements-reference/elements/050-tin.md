# Element 50: Tin

**Symbol:** Sn  
**Atomic number:** 50  
**Group / period / block:** 14 / 5 / p  
**Standard atomic weight:** 118.710(7)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s² 5p²  
**Ground-state term:** ³P₀  
**First ionization energy:** 7.3439 eV  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid (white β-Sn is the stable allotrope near room temperature at ambient pressure)

**Evidence status:** Initial research draft; values and allotrope boundaries require final source audit.

## 1. Atomic and quantum properties
Tin has a 5s²5p² valence shell outside a closed [Kr]4d¹⁰ core. The listed ground term is ³P₀. The compact configuration, term, and first ionization energy are reported in the NIST periodic-table compilation. A full account must use NIST ASD for the fine-structure levels, transition data, isotope shifts, and uncertainty metadata; the term symbol alone is not a complete electronic-structure description.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopic composition
CIAAW standard atomic weight is 118.710(7). Natural tin has ten listed isotopes: ¹¹²Sn 0.0097(1), ¹¹⁴Sn 0.0066(1), ¹¹⁵Sn 0.0034(1), ¹¹⁶Sn 0.1454(9), ¹¹⁷Sn 0.0768(7), ¹¹⁸Sn 0.2422(9), ¹¹⁹Sn 0.0859(4), ¹²⁰Sn 0.3258(9), ¹²²Sn 0.0463(3), and ¹²⁴Sn 0.0579(5). These are CIAAW representative amount fractions; isotope composition can vary among samples. Nuclear spins, excited states, decay properties of radioactive tin nuclides, and neutron-capture cross sections require ENSDF and evaluated nuclear-data libraries.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
Tin is solid at 20 °C and 1 bar. Two allotropes are central: white β-Sn, a metallic tetragonal phase stable near room temperature, and grey α-Sn, a diamond-cubic semiconducting phase stable below the transition temperature near 13.2 °C for sufficiently pure tin at ambient pressure. The transformation is kinetically sensitive and can be affected by impurities, stress, and nucleation; the familiar term “tin pest” describes disintegration associated with the α transformation. PubChem's periodic-table compilation lists a melting point of 505.08 K for tin; this is a compiled value, and allotrope-specific thermodynamic context must be retained in a final dataset.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The common oxidation states are +2 and +4. Sn(II) can act as a reducing agent and is often susceptible to oxidation to Sn(IV); Sn(IV) forms stable oxides and halide complexes. Tin(II) and tin(IV) exhibit distinct hydrolysis, coordination, and redox chemistry. Organotin compounds form a chemically diverse class with uses and hazards that vary greatly by structure; it is scientifically incorrect to assign one toxicological profile to all organotin species.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Tin is used in solder alloys, tinplate, bronze and other alloys, chemical coatings, and semiconductor materials. Tin-based solders require phase-diagram and intermetallic-compound treatment, especially in Sn-Ag-Cu systems and at solder/substrate interfaces. Tin dioxide is a functional oxide used in gas sensors and transparent or catalytic coatings. Organotin compounds have specialised industrial uses, although many applications have been restricted due to environmental toxicity.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
Inorganic tin compounds generally have different hazard profiles from organotin compounds. Certain organotin species are highly toxic, including compounds with substantial neurotoxic, immunotoxic, reproductive, or aquatic effects. Tin dust and fumes can pose occupational inhalation hazards. Risk assessment must specify oxidation state, ligand set, solubility, particle form, and exposure route; avoid treating elemental tin, Sn(II), Sn(IV), and organotin compounds as equivalent.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Tin has been used since antiquity, notably in bronze, an alloy of copper and tin. The symbol Sn derives from Latin *stannum*. Its allotropy and the tin-pest transformation became important examples in the development of solid-state chemistry and materials thermodynamics.
