# Element 96: Curium

**Symbol:** Cm  
**Atomic number:** 96  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [247]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f⁷ 6d 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁹D°₂  
**First ionization energy:** 5.992241 eV (NIST ASD 5.12)  
**Important isotopes:** ²⁴⁴Cm, ²⁴⁵Cm, ²⁴⁶Cm, ²⁴⁷Cm and ²⁴⁸Cm  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear, radiothermal and material-property datasets require specialist evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Curium is a transuranium actinoid with neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f⁷6d7s² and ground term ⁹D°₂, according to the NIST atomic-data evaluation. The common oxidation state is +3, while +4 is also established in selected compounds. The half-filled 5f shell contributes to its high-spin electronic structure. Curium's 5f orbitals participate in bonding and are influenced by relativistic effects and strong electron correlation.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Curium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight; [247] is a representative mass number. Important isotopes include ²⁴⁴Cm (half-life about 18.1 years), ²⁴⁵Cm (about 8.48×10³ years), ²⁴⁶Cm (about 4.76×10³ years), ²⁴⁷Cm (about 1.56×10⁷ years) and ²⁴⁸Cm (about 3.48×10⁵ years). Several isotopes are alpha emitters; spontaneous-fission branches also exist for some nuclides. ²⁴⁴Cm has substantial decay heat and has been used in specialised radioisotope power research. Isotope-specific decay constants and emission probabilities must be taken from current ENSDF/NuDat evaluations.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
Curium is a silvery radioactive metal that tarnishes in air. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1345 °C and density 13.51 g cm⁻³; a reliable boiling point is not established in the same compilation. Physical properties are difficult to measure because only very small quantities are available and radioactive decay produces heat and radiation damage. The sample isotope, age, phase, purity and containment should be recorded for any material measurement.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Curium commonly forms +3 compounds, including Cm₂O₃ and trivalent halides; +4 is accessible in selected oxide and fluoride systems. Curium(III) coordination chemistry resembles that of neighbouring actinoids but remains less extensively studied because of supply and radiological constraints. Hydrolysis, complexation and adsorption depend on pH, ligand environment and ionic strength.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Curium has limited use outside specialised research. ²⁴⁴Cm has been investigated as a compact heat source for radioisotope power systems, and curium isotopes have been used in alpha-particle X-ray spectrometry for elemental analysis in space research. These are specialised, isotope-specific applications and are not ordinary industrial uses of bulk metal. Curium is also important as a target material in the synthesis of heavier actinoids.

## 6. Radiological safety
Curium is highly radioactive and radiotoxic. Alpha-emitting isotopes pose a major internal contamination hazard through inhalation, ingestion or wounds. Some isotopes also have significant spontaneous-fission neutron emissions. Handling requires licensed radiological facilities, engineered containment, remote handling where appropriate, contamination monitoring and isotope-specific dosimetry.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Curium was first synthesised in 1944 by Glenn Seaborg, Ralph James and Albert Ghiorso at Berkeley by bombarding plutonium with alpha particles. It was named in honour of Marie and Pierre Curie for their pioneering work on radioactivity.
