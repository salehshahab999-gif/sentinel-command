# Element 93: Neptunium

**Symbol:** Np  
**Atomic number:** 93  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [237]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f⁴ 6d 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁶L₁₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.2655 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Important isotope:** ²³⁷Np  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear, aqueous-speciation and material-property datasets require further evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Neptunium is the first transuranium element and has neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f⁴6d7s² with ground term ⁶L₁₁/₂. It exhibits a wide range of oxidation states, including +3, +4, +5, +6 and +7. The relative stability of these states depends strongly on redox conditions and ligand environment. Np(V) commonly occurs as the neptunyl ion NpO₂⁺, while Np(VI) forms NpO₂²⁺. This redox flexibility is important in actinide separation and environmental transport.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Neptunium has no stable isotopes and therefore no standard atomic weight. The bracketed mass number [237] is a representative value. ²³⁷Np has a half-life of approximately 2.144×10⁶ years and is an alpha emitter. It is generated in nuclear reactors and can occur in trace quantities in uranium ores through neutron-capture and decay pathways. Other Np isotopes are shorter-lived and are relevant to specialised nuclear studies. Half-lives, decay modes and emission probabilities must be taken from current ENSDF/NuDat evaluations.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
Neptunium is a radioactive silvery metal with complex allotropy. The RSC compilation lists melting point 644 °C, boiling point 3902 °C and density 20.2 g cm⁻³. Neptunium exhibits several crystalline phases and unusual volume changes with temperature. These properties are sensitive to phase, impurity content and sample history, and direct bulk measurements require specialist radiological facilities.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Neptunium chemistry is distinguished by multiple stable or metastable oxidation states in aqueous environments. Np(IV) is generally less soluble under many conditions than Np(V), while Np(V) neptunyl species can be more mobile in oxidising groundwater. Np(VI) forms the dioxocation NpO₂²⁺. Hydrolysis, carbonate complexation, redox potential, pH and mineral adsorption are important controls on environmental mobility. A meaningful speciation record must define the chemical conditions.

## 5. Research and applications
Neptunium has limited applications outside nuclear science. ²³⁷Np has been used in specialised neutron-detection and nuclear-measurement contexts. Neptunium chemistry is important in understanding long-lived actinide behaviour in spent-fuel systems, environmental transport and radioactive-waste management. This scientific overview does not include process instructions for separation or production.

## 6. Radiological safety and environmental considerations
Neptunium is radioactive and chemically toxic. Internal exposure by inhalation or ingestion can deliver alpha radiation and long-term organ dose. Its mobility depends on oxidation state and environmental ligands, so containment and waste assessment must include chemical speciation as well as activity. Handling requires licensed facilities, contamination control and isotope-specific dosimetry.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Neptunium was discovered in 1940 by Edwin McMillan and Philip Abelson at the University of California, Berkeley, following neutron irradiation of uranium. It was named after Neptune, the planet following Uranus, reflecting its position after uranium in the periodic table.
