# Element 106: Seaborgium

**Symbol:** Sg  
**Atomic number:** 106  
**Group / period / block:** 6 / 7 / d  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [269]  
**Predicted/compiled ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 6d⁴ 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁵D₀  
**First ionization energy:** approximately 7.8 eV (PDG 2024 review of NIST/atomic-theory data)  
**Representative isotopes:** ²⁶⁹Sg and ²⁷¹Sg  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; chemistry is based on single-atom experiments and bulk physical properties are not measured.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Seaborgium is a transactinoid in group 6, below chromium, molybdenum and tungsten. Its predicted neutral ground configuration is [Rn]5f¹⁴6d⁴7s², with ground term ⁵D₀. The +6 oxidation state is expected to be important, consistent with group-6 chemistry. Relativistic effects influence orbital energies and chemical behaviour; comparison with tungsten requires attention to differences in solvation, volatility and ligand bonding.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Seaborgium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight. [269] is a representative mass number. CIAAW lists ²⁶⁹Sg with a half-life of about 5(2) minutes and ²⁷¹Sg with about 2.2(1.1) minutes. These very short half-lives constrain chemical experiments to rapid transport and detection. Exact decay branches, alpha energies and daughter nuclides must be checked against current ENSDF/NuDat and NUBASE evaluations.

## 3. Physical properties and measurement limits
No macroscopic sample of seaborgium has been produced. Density, melting and boiling points, crystal structure, hardness and electrical conductivity are not experimentally established. Any values in periodic-table projections are theoretical estimates, not measured physical constants. Single-atom experiments do not provide direct bulk thermodynamic properties.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Trace chemical experiments indicate that seaborgium can form volatile oxochloride species and behaves broadly as a group-6 element, with similarities to molybdenum and tungsten. The expected +6 state and formation of oxyhalides are important for its gas-phase chemistry. The very short half-life and few-atom production regime place significant limits on equilibrium interpretation and uncertainty estimation.

## 5. Research relevance
Seaborgium has no commercial or technological use. Its importance lies in tests of relativistic chemistry, group-6 periodic trends and the chemical boundary of the transactinoid region. It is produced in specialised accelerator facilities and studied through rapid chemical separation coupled to radioactive decay detection.

## 6. Radiological safety
Seaborgium isotopes are radioactive and extremely short-lived. Work is restricted to specialised nuclear-research facilities using licensed containment and radiological controls. The main practical concerns are target and separator contamination, radioactive daughter products and isotope-specific alpha-radiation exposure.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Seaborgium was discovered in 1974 through research at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna. Its name honours Glenn T. Seaborg, a central figure in actinoid and transuranium chemistry. IUPAC accepted the name in 1997 after international review.
