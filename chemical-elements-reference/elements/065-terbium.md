# Element 65: Terbium

**Symbol:** Tb  
**Atomic number:** 65  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 158.925 354(7)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f⁹ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁶H°₁₅/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 5.8638 eV (NIST)  
**Naturally occurring nuclide:** ¹⁵⁹Tb (monoisotopic; amount fraction 1)  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; detailed spectroscopy, magnetic phase data and materials-property review remain open.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Terbium is a heavy lanthanoid with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f⁹6s² and ground term ⁶H°₁₅/₂. Its 4f electrons are relatively localized and strongly shielded by outer 5s and 5p shells, giving rise to pronounced multiplet structure and large orbital contributions to magnetic behaviour. Tb³⁺ is the dominant oxidation state in ordinary compounds. Tb⁴⁺ is also established in selected oxides, fluorides and other strongly oxidising solid-state environments; its stability depends on lattice and ligand chemistry.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear properties
Terbium is monoisotopic in nature: ¹⁵⁹Tb has representative amount fraction 1 and an evaluated atomic mass of 158.925354(7) Da. CIAAW derives the standard atomic weight directly from this nuclide. ¹⁵⁹Tb has nuclear spin I = 3/2. Although it is the sole naturally occurring stable isotope, many radioactive terbium nuclides are produced in reactors, accelerators or nuclear reactions. Their half-lives, decay branches, gamma emissions and uncertainties must be taken from current ENSDF/NuDat evaluations; they should not be inferred from the stable natural isotope entry.

## 3. Physical and magnetic properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar terbium is a solid, soft, silvery metal with a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation lists a melting point of 1359 °C, boiling point of 3230 °C and density of 8.23 g cm⁻³. Terbium metal exhibits strong temperature-dependent magnetic ordering. A single magnetic classification or ordering temperature is insufficient: phase, field, crystallographic orientation, purity and measurement protocol must be specified. The material's large magnetocrystalline anisotropy and magnetostriction are important in functional alloys.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The common oxidation states are +3 and +4. Tb³⁺ chemistry resembles that of neighbouring lanthanoids, with high coordination numbers and strong affinity for oxygen-donor ligands. Tb₂O₃ contains Tb(III), whereas TbO₂ contains formally tetravalent terbium. Mixed-valence oxides and fluorides require careful treatment of charge balance, defects and electronic structure. In aqueous solution, Tb(III) is the usual stable state under ordinary conditions; redox behaviour depends on pH and ligand environment.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Tb³⁺ is a major green-emitting activator in phosphors used in solid-state lighting, displays and optical devices. Terbium compounds are used in phosphors based on calcium fluoride, calcium tungstate and related hosts. A terbium-iron alloy, together with dysprosium in Terfenol-D (typically a Tb-Dy-Fe composition), exhibits giant magnetostriction: it changes dimensions under an applied magnetic field. This underpins specialised actuators, transducers and acoustic devices. The effect belongs to the engineered alloy and its texture/composition, not to an unspecified pure-terbium sample.

## 6. Safety and environmental notes
Terbium has no established biological role. The toxicity profile depends on compound, solubility, particle size and exposure route; limited toxicological data should not be interpreted as proof of zero hazard. Rare-earth refining and phosphor manufacture require dust control and appropriate waste management.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Terbium was isolated by Carl Gustaf Mosander in 1843 during the investigation of yttria-containing material. Its name derives from Ytterby, Sweden, a locality associated with several rare-earth discoveries. The historical interchange of the names terbium and erbium reflects the difficulty of separating rare-earth fractions in the nineteenth century.
