# Element 118: Oganesson

**Symbol:** Og  
**Atomic number:** 118  
**Group / period / block:** 18 / 7 / p  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [294]  
**Predicted ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 6d¹⁰ 7s² 7p⁶  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** not experimentally established; theoretical calculations are model-dependent  
**Representative isotope:** ²⁹⁴Og  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; all chemical and bulk physical properties remain theoretical or unknown.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Oganesson is the heaviest confirmed element and occupies group 18. Its predicted neutral ground configuration is [Rn]5f¹⁴6d¹⁰7s²7p⁶, with closed-shell term ¹S₀. Relativistic effects are exceptionally strong and may cause significant deviations from the behaviour of lighter noble gases. The filled p shell does not guarantee that oganesson is an inert gas under ordinary conditions; theoretical studies predict unusually strong dispersion interactions and potentially substantial solid-state character.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Oganesson has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight. [294] is a representative mass number. CIAAW/NUBASE2020 list ²⁹⁴Og with a half-life of 0.7(3) milliseconds. The isotope is identified through correlated decay products because only a few atoms have ever been produced. Exact nuclear level, decay and daughter-chain information must be taken from current ENSDF/NuDat.

## 3. Physical properties and measurement limits
No macroscopic sample of oganesson has been produced. RSC lists melting point, boiling point and density as unknown. Some theoretical models predict that oganesson may be a solid at room temperature because of its high polarizability and strong interatomic interactions, but this has not been experimentally observed. No measured crystal structure, thermal conductivity, elastic constants or vapour pressure is available.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Oganesson is expected to have group-18 chemistry, but relativistic calculations predict greater polarizability and potentially stronger chemical interactions than in lighter noble gases. The possibility of compounds and unusual oxidation states has been discussed theoretically, but no experimentally validated compound or ordinary chemical reaction has been demonstrated. Its label as a “noble gas” is a periodic classification, not a guarantee of complete chemical inertness.

## 5. Research relevance
Oganesson has no commercial or technological application. Its importance is in nuclear stability, relativistic quantum chemistry and the study of the predicted island of stability in superheavy nuclei. Production and detection require specialised heavy-ion facilities and identification through radioactive decay chains.

## 6. Radiological safety
Oganesson is extremely radioactive and short-lived. Work is confined to specialised accelerator laboratories with licensed containment and radiation-safety controls. Because only a few atoms are produced, no conventional handling or occupational exposure dataset exists.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Oganesson was first produced in experiments at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna in collaboration with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. IUPAC approved the name in November 2016 in honour of Yuri Oganessian, whose research made major contributions to the discovery of superheavy elements.
