# Element 97: Berkelium

**Symbol:** Bk  
**Atomic number:** 97  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [247]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f⁹ 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁶H°₁₅/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.1979 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Important isotopes:** ²⁴⁷Bk and ²⁴⁹Bk  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear, spectroscopic and compound-property data are necessarily limited by extreme scarcity.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Berkelium is a synthetic actinoid with neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f⁹7s² and ground term ⁶H°₁₅/₂. The +3 oxidation state is common, while +4 is also chemically accessible and useful in actinoid separation chemistry. The relative stability of Bk(III) and Bk(IV) is influenced by the 5f electron configuration and ligand environment. Its atomic spectrum is based on very limited quantities and requires specialist NIST ASD data for detailed level assignments.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Berkelium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight; [247] is a representative mass number. ²⁴⁷Bk has a half-life of approximately 1,380 years, while ²⁴⁹Bk has a half-life of about 330 days. Both are radioactive and have isotope-specific decay chains. The exact decay branches, emissions and evaluated uncertainties must be obtained from current ENSDF/NuDat; rounded half-lives in general reference tables are orientation values only.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
Berkelium is a radioactive, silvery metal. The RSC compilation lists melting point 986 °C and density 14.78 g cm⁻³; a boiling point is not established in the same fact box. Bulk measurements are very limited because only minute quantities are produced, and self-irradiation and surface oxidation can affect samples. Physical properties should be labelled measured, estimated or extrapolated and should include isotope and sample-age metadata.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Berkelium forms compounds primarily in +3 and +4 oxidation states. Bk(III) resembles other trivalent actinoids, while Bk(IV) can be stabilised in selected oxide and fluoride compounds. Its chemical behaviour is important for understanding periodic trends across the actinoid series and for separating berkelium from curium and californium. Hydrolysis and complexation depend on oxidation state, pH and ligand environment.

## 5. Research relevance
Berkelium has no commercial or established technological application. Its principal importance is in fundamental actinoid chemistry and as a target nuclide in the synthesis of heavier elements, including tennessine. Only very small quantities have been produced, so experiments require highly specialised facilities and careful radiochemical accounting.

## 6. Radiological safety
Berkelium is radioactive and requires licensed radiochemical handling. Internal contamination is a key hazard because alpha-emitting decay products can deliver dose to tissues. Exposure prevention, containment, contamination monitoring, waste control and isotope-specific dosimetry are essential. Its rarity does not reduce the hazard of a prepared source.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Berkelium was first synthesised in December 1949 by Stanley Thompson, Albert Ghiorso and Glenn Seaborg at the University of California, Berkeley. It was named after the city of Berkeley, in parallel with terbium's name from Ytterby.
