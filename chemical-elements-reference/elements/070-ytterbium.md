# Element 70: Ytterbium

**Symbol:** Yb  
**Atomic number:** 70  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 173.045(10)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 6.25416 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁶⁸Yb, ¹⁷⁰Yb, ¹⁷¹Yb, ¹⁷²Yb, ¹⁷³Yb, ¹⁷⁴Yb and ¹⁷⁶Yb  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific nuclear, optical-clock and laser datasets require further evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Ytterbium has a closed 4f shell in the neutral ground state, [Xe]4f¹⁴6s², with term ¹S₀. Both +2 and +3 oxidation states are important. Yb²⁺ retains the closed-shell 4f¹⁴ configuration, which helps stabilise divalent compounds, while Yb³⁺ has a 4f¹³ configuration and is common in aqueous and oxide chemistry. This accessible divalent/trivalent redox chemistry distinguishes ytterbium from many neighbouring lanthanoids. The closed-shell neutral ground state also makes ytterbium attractive for precision spectroscopy and optical-clock experiments.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative amount fractions are: ¹⁶⁸Yb 0.00126(1), ¹⁷⁰Yb 0.03023(2), ¹⁷¹Yb 0.14216(7), ¹⁷²Yb 0.21754(10), ¹⁷³Yb 0.16098(9), ¹⁷⁴Yb 0.31896(26) and ¹⁷⁶Yb 0.12887(30). CIAAW gives the standard atomic weight 173.045(10). ¹⁷¹Yb and ¹⁷³Yb have non-zero nuclear spin and are relevant to hyperfine spectroscopy; the even-mass isotopes have zero ground-state spin. ¹⁷⁶Yb is an extremely long-lived double-beta-decay nuclide. Exact decay half-life and mode assignments should be taken from a current ENSDF/NuDat evaluation.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
At room temperature ytterbium metal adopts a face-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 824 °C, boiling point 1196 °C and density 6.90 g cm⁻³. Ytterbium is relatively soft and ductile and slowly oxidises in air, forming a surface oxide. Its phase behaviour changes with pressure and temperature, so any crystallographic or mechanical value must state the experimental conditions.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Ytterbium(III) is common in oxides, halides and coordination compounds, while Yb(II) is well established in selected halides, chalcogenides and organometallic compounds. The Yb²⁺/Yb³⁺ redox couple is sensitive to solvent, counterion and ligand environment. Yb₂O₃ is the common trivalent oxide; divalent ytterbium compounds require suitable reducing or lattice-stabilising conditions. Hydrolysis and aqueous speciation depend on pH and ligand competition.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Ytterbium-doped fibre and solid-state lasers are used in industrial cutting, welding, marking and high-power optical systems. Yb:YAG and ytterbium-doped silica are important laser gain media. Selected ytterbium isotopes are also used in optical lattice clocks and precision tests of atomic structure; clock performance depends on a specified isotope, transition, lattice wavelength and systematic-shift evaluation. Ytterbium compounds are investigated for catalysts, optical materials, memory devices and near-infrared luminescence. These properties belong to defined compounds or atomic transitions, not to generic bulk ytterbium.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Ytterbium has no established biological role. Bulk metal is reactive, and powders require appropriate controls. Compound hazards depend on solubility, particle size and exposure route; limited toxicity data should not be treated as proof of harmlessness. Rare-earth mining, refining and end-of-life recovery can generate chemically complex wastes.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Ytterbium was identified by Jean-Charles Galissard de Marignac in 1878 in material from Ytterby, Sweden. Its name derives from the locality that also gave names to several other rare-earth elements. The element's separation history reflects the close chemical similarity of lanthanoids.
