# Element 82: Lead

**Symbol:** Pb  
**Atomic number:** 82  
**Group / period / block:** 14 / 6 / p  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** [206.14, 207.94]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s² 6p²  
**Ground-state term:** ³P₀  
**First ionization energy:** 7.4167 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ²⁰⁴Pb, ²⁰⁶Pb, ²⁰⁷Pb and ²⁰⁸Pb  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotopic-range interpretation, toxicology and lead-isotope geochronology require detailed evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Lead is a heavy group-14 p-block metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s²6p² and ground term ³P₀. The inert-pair effect stabilises the +2 oxidation state relative to +4, especially in many inorganic compounds. Pb(IV) compounds are known but are generally stronger oxidants and may be reduced to Pb(II). Relativistic effects contribute to the stability of the 6s² electron pair and influence bonding across the heavy p block.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
Natural lead contains four primordial nuclides: ²⁰⁴Pb, ²⁰⁶Pb, ²⁰⁷Pb and ²⁰⁸Pb. Their representative amount fractions vary substantially among normal terrestrial materials because ²⁰⁶Pb, ²⁰⁷Pb and ²⁰⁸Pb are radiogenic daughter products of uranium and thorium decay chains. CIAAW therefore gives an interval standard atomic weight, [206.14, 207.94], rather than a single universal value. The variable isotope ratios underpin Pb-Pb and U-Pb geochronology and provenance studies. A sample's measured isotope ratio can preserve information on source rocks, age and geochemical evolution.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At ambient conditions lead is a soft, dense, bluish-silver metal with a face-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 327.462 °C, boiling point 1749 °C and density 11.3 g cm⁻³. Lead is easily cast and formed and has useful attenuation for X-rays and gamma rays. Creep, corrosion and mechanical strength depend on alloying and temperature. Isotopic composition and phase history should be reported for high-precision reference materials.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The common oxidation states are +2 and +4, with Pb(II) dominant in many ordinary aqueous environments. PbO, PbO₂, lead sulfide and lead carbonate are important compounds and minerals. Lead forms sparingly soluble salts such as PbSO₄ and PbCO₃, but solubility and bioavailability depend strongly on pH, complexing ligands and particle form. In natural waters and soils, adsorption to minerals and organic matter can control mobility. Total lead concentration alone is not a complete risk or speciation assessment.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Lead remains widely used in lead-acid batteries, radiation shielding, cable sheathing, selected solders and specialised glass. Many former uses, including leaded petrol, lead paint and lead-containing plumbing systems, have been restricted or eliminated because of health risks. Battery recycling is a major source of secondary lead and requires rigorous controls to prevent occupational and environmental contamination.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
Lead is a cumulative toxicant with no known beneficial biological role. Exposure can affect the nervous system, kidneys, blood formation, cardiovascular function and reproduction. Children and developing fetuses are particularly vulnerable to neurodevelopmental effects. There is no established safe exposure threshold for many health outcomes. Risk depends on chemical form, route, dose, duration and age; inhaled dust/fume and ingested contaminated material are important pathways. Workplaces and recycling operations require strict dust control, hygiene and environmental monitoring.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Lead has been used since antiquity and was worked by multiple ancient civilisations. The symbol Pb derives from Latin *plumbum*. Historical use in water pipes, pigments, cookware and fuel additives is now recognised as a major source of public-health exposure.
