# Element 35: Bromine

**Symbol:** Br  
**Atomic number:** 35  
**Group / period / block:** 17 / 4 / p  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** [79.901, 79.907]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵  
**Ground-state term:** ²P°₃/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 11.8138 eV (PDG 2025 review of NIST data)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Liquid  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-abundance ranges and compound-specific toxicology require careful treatment.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Bromine is a halogen with neutral ground configuration [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p⁵ and ground term ²P°₃/₂. Its valence shell contains one vacancy relative to a closed p subshell, giving it strong electron-accepting and oxidising behaviour. The common oxidation state in bromides is −1; positive oxidation states occur in oxo-compounds such as bromates and perbromates. Spin-orbit coupling splits the atomic ground configuration into fine-structure levels.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
Natural bromine contains two stable isotopes, ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br. CIAAW gives representative amount-fraction ranges of [0.505, 0.508] for ⁷⁹Br and [0.492, 0.495] for ⁸¹Br. Because normal terrestrial isotope composition varies, CIAAW assigns the interval standard atomic weight [79.901, 79.907], not a single exact value. Both isotopes have nuclear spin and are important in bromine NMR and isotope-ratio studies. Radioactive bromine isotopes are produced in nuclear reactions and require isotope-specific ENSDF/NuDat data.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar bromine is a dense, reddish-brown volatile liquid. The RSC compilation lists melting point about −7.2 °C, boiling point 58.8 °C and liquid density about 3.12 g cm⁻³ near room temperature. Bromine evaporates readily and its vapour is corrosive and hazardous. Vapour pressure and liquid density vary significantly with temperature; reference values should always include the stated temperature and pressure.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Bromine exists as diatomic Br₂ in its elemental liquid and vapour phases. It is an oxidising agent and reacts with many metals and organic compounds. Bromide, Br⁻, is common in salts and natural waters; bromine also forms oxyanions in positive oxidation states. Organobromine compounds are diverse and include pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, flame-retardant chemicals and intermediates. Their environmental persistence and hazard depend strongly on molecular structure.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Bromine compounds are used in flame-retardant formulations, water treatment, pharmaceuticals, photographic chemistry and selected drilling fluids. Use of particular brominated flame retardants has been restricted or phased out where persistence, bioaccumulation or toxicity are concerns. Elemental bromine is also an important chemical intermediate in industrial synthesis. The properties and safety of individual brominated compounds must not be generalised from elemental Br₂.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Elemental bromine is corrosive and toxic. Its vapour can severely irritate and damage the respiratory tract, eyes and skin. Handling requires closed systems, suitable chemical-resistant containment, ventilation and emergency controls. Bromide salts are generally much less hazardous than elemental bromine, while organobromine compounds have widely varying persistence and toxicity profiles.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Bromine was discovered independently by Carl Jacob Löwig and Antoine Jérôme Balard in 1825–1826. Its name derives from Greek *brōmos*, meaning stench, referring to its strong unpleasant odour.
