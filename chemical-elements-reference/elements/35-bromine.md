# Element 35: Bromine

**Symbol:** Br  
**Atomic number:** 35  
**Group:** 17  
**Period:** 4  
**Block:** p-block  
**Classification:** Halogen  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** [79.901, 79.907]  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Bromine is a period-4 p-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵. The periodic classification is useful for orientation, but chemical properties must be interpreted in terms of electronic structure, phase, oxidation state, and reaction conditions.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Natural bromine consists of two stable isotopes, ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br. CIAAW lists isotope masses 78.918 338(7) Da and 80.916 288(6) Da, with representative amount-fraction intervals [0.505, 0.508] and [0.492, 0.495]. The standard atomic weight is an interval, [79.901, 79.907], reflecting natural isotopic variation.

## 3. Physical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar, elemental bromine is a dense, volatile, red-brown liquid. RSC reference values are melting point 266 K, boiling point 332 K, and density 3.1028 g cm⁻³. Vapour pressure and temperature are particularly important because bromine readily produces a toxic vapour.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Bromine is a halogen and commonly occurs in oxidation state −1 in bromides. Positive oxidation states, especially +1, +3, +5, and +7, occur in oxycompounds and interhalogen chemistry. Molecular bromine is Br₂; it participates in electrophilic addition and substitution reactions and forms hydrogen bromide and a wide range of inorganic and organic bromides.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Bromine compounds are used in chemical synthesis, pharmaceuticals, photographic chemistry, drilling fluids, and selected flame-retardant systems. Some legacy brominated flame retardants have been restricted because of persistence, bioaccumulation, or toxicity; the environmental profile is compound-specific and should not be generalized to elemental bromine.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Elemental bromine is corrosive and toxic by inhalation and skin or eye contact. Its vapour can cause severe respiratory injury. Handle only with suitable containment, ventilation, compatible materials, and appropriate protective equipment. Toxicological and regulatory classifications differ substantially among bromide salts and organobromine compounds.
