# Element 39: Yttrium

**Symbol:** Y  
**Atomic number:** 39  
**Group:** 3  
**Period:** 5  
**Block:** d-block  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW 2024):** 88.905 838(2)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 4d¹ 5s²  
**Ground-state term (NIST tabulation):** ²D₃/₂  
**First ionization energy (NIST tabulation):** 6.2173 eV  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Yttrium is a period-5 d-block transition metal. Its ground-state configuration is [Kr] 4d¹ 5s². The tabulated ground-state term and first ionization energy are reproduced from the NIST periodic-table compilation; the current NIST Atomic Spectra Database should be used for a full level-by-level spectroscopic treatment.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear data
Natural yttrium is effectively monoisotopic: ⁸⁹Y has a representative amount fraction of 1. Its standard atomic weight is therefore derived from the atomic mass of ⁸⁹Y. Radioisotopes, including ⁹⁰Y, are produced in nuclear reactions and are not constituents of ordinary natural yttrium in relevant abundance.

**Evidence status:** Standard atomic weight and representative isotope abundances are attributed to CIAAW's current tables. This monograph is not a complete evaluated nuclide chart. Half-lives, decay branches, Q-values, radiation energies, and uncertainties for all radioactive isotopes require isotope-by-isotope verification against ENSDF.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
- **Melting point:** 1795 K (1522 °C)
- **Boiling point:** 3618 K (3345 °C)
- **Density:** 4.47 g cm⁻³ (RSC reference value; temperature and pressure conventions should be checked against the underlying handbook before precision engineering use)
- **Crystal structure at ambient conditions:** At ambient conditions yttrium adopts a hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure. Phase stability and properties depend on purity, temperature, pressure, and hydrogen/oxygen contamination.

The values above are reference values, not a critical evaluation of all measurements. High-temperature phase transitions, purity, defects, and interstitial contamination can materially alter measured behaviour.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Yttrium is a reactive, electropositive transition metal with +3 as its dominant oxidation state. It forms Y₂O₃, YF₃, YCl₃ and a broad family of oxides, halides, and coordination compounds. Y³⁺ chemistry often resembles that of the heavier rare-earth ions; the metal is protected to some extent by an oxide film.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Yttria-stabilized zirconia is a major oxygen-ion-conducting ceramic used in sensors, solid-oxide fuel cells, and thermal-barrier systems. Yttrium is also important in YAG laser crystals, phosphors, optical ceramics, microwave filters, and selected high-temperature alloys. Y-90 is used in targeted radionuclide therapy, but its medical applications concern the isotope, not bulk stable yttrium.

## 6. Toxicology, safety, and handling
Bulk yttrium metal can react with air and moisture, while finely divided powder may present a fire hazard. Soluble yttrium compounds require exposure controls; biological effects depend on chemical form and dose. Radioactive ⁹⁰Y requires radiological controls distinct from stable ⁸⁹Y.
