# Element 76: Osmium

**Symbol:** Os  
**Atomic number:** 76  
**Group / period / block:** 8 / 6 / d  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 190.23(3)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d⁶ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁵D₄  
**First ionization energy:** 8.43823 eV (NIST)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁸⁴Os, ¹⁸⁶Os, ¹⁸⁷Os, ¹⁸⁸Os, ¹⁸⁹Os, ¹⁹⁰Os and ¹⁹²Os  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope geochemistry, nuclear evaluation and osmium tetroxide hazard controls require further source-level review.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Osmium is a group-8 transition metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d⁶6s² and ground term ⁵D₄. Its chemistry includes oxidation states from low-valent organometallic complexes to high-valent oxides, especially +4 and +8. Osmium tetroxide, OsO₄, is a volatile molecular oxide and a powerful oxidising reagent. The electronic and bonding descriptions of high-valent osmium compounds require molecular and coordination-chemistry treatment rather than simple ionic models alone.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative isotopic amount fractions are: ¹⁸⁴Os 0.0002(2), ¹⁸⁶Os 0.0159(64), ¹⁸⁷Os 0.0196(17), ¹⁸⁸Os 0.1324(27), ¹⁸⁹Os 0.1615(23), ¹⁹⁰Os 0.2626(20) and ¹⁹²Os 0.4078(32). The standard atomic weight is 190.23(3). The abundance of ¹⁸⁷Os is influenced by radiogenic production from ¹⁸⁷Re decay, making Os isotopic composition important in Re–Os geochronology and mantle geochemistry. ¹⁸⁶Os is radioactive and undergoes α-decay to stable ¹⁸²W, with a half-life of approximately 2.0 × 10¹⁵ years. Although ¹⁸⁴Os is extremely rare, searches for its decay have reported channel-specific lower limits rather than a confirmed decay. Isotope-specific masses, decay schemes and uncertainties should be sourced from CIAAW and ENSDF/NuDat.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At ambient conditions osmium has a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation gives melting point 3033 °C, boiling point 5008 °C and density 22.5872 g cm⁻³. It is commonly described as the densest naturally occurring element by room-temperature density, although very small differences with iridium and the adopted reference conditions should be kept in view. Osmium is hard, brittle and chemically resistant in bulk form. Mechanical properties and density must be tied to phase, purity and temperature.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Osmium exhibits a broad range of oxidation states. Os(IV) and Os(VIII) are particularly important; OsO₄ contains osmium in the +8 state and is volatile, strongly oxidising and highly toxic. Osmium forms complexes with carbon monoxide, phosphines, alkenes, hydrides and other ligands, supporting specialised catalytic cycles. The chemistry of osmium requires careful control of redox potential and ligand environment.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Osmium has limited bulk applications because it is rare, brittle and difficult to work. It is used in very hard alloys and specialised contacts, pivots and instrument components. Osmium compounds and supported osmium catalysts are used in selected organic transformations. OsO₄ has long been used as a stain and contrast reagent in electron microscopy because it reacts with unsaturated lipids, but its volatility and severe toxicity require tightly controlled handling and containment.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
The key safety distinction is between bulk osmium metal and osmium tetroxide. OsO₄ is volatile, can damage eyes, skin and respiratory tissues, and presents a serious inhalation hazard. Work with OsO₄ requires closed handling, appropriate containment, exposure controls and validated waste treatment. Bulk metal should not be described as having the same hazard profile as its volatile oxide. All osmium waste streams should be segregated and managed according to compound form.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Osmium was discovered with iridium by Smithson Tennant in 1803 while investigating the insoluble residue from aqua-regia treatment of crude platinum. Its name derives from the Greek *osmē*, meaning smell, referring to the strong odour associated with some osmium oxide chemistry.
