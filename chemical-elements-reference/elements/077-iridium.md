# Element 77: Iridium

**Symbol:** Ir  
**Atomic number:** 77  
**Group / period / block:** 9 / 6 / d  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 192.217(2)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d⁷ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁴F₉/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 8.96702 eV (NIST)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁹¹Ir and ¹⁹³Ir  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; catalytic, electrochemical and isotope-geochemistry data require additional evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Iridium is a group-9 transition metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d⁷6s² and ground term ⁴F₉/₂. Common oxidation states include +3 and +4, while a wider range is known in specialised complexes and oxides. Strong relativistic effects influence the electronic structure and bonding of this heavy 5d element. Iridium is notable for its high corrosion resistance and the stability of selected high-valent oxides and coordination compounds.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative amount fractions are ¹⁹¹Ir 0.3723(9) and ¹⁹³Ir 0.6277(9), with standard atomic weight 192.217(2). Both naturally occurring isotopes are stable and have nuclear spin I = 3/2. Isotope-sensitive spectroscopy and nuclear magnetic resonance require the appropriate hyperfine and nuclear-moment data. Radioactive iridium nuclides are produced in nuclear reactions and must be described using isotope-specific evaluated nuclear data.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar iridium is a hard, silvery metal with a face-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 2446 °C, boiling point 4428 °C and density 22.5622 g cm⁻³. It has exceptional resistance to corrosion and oxidation relative to many metals. Mechanical properties depend on grain structure, impurities, temperature and deformation history; high melting point alone does not fully describe its high-temperature performance.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Iridium forms compounds in several oxidation states, commonly +3 and +4, and also higher-valent oxides and complexes. IrO₂ is a conductive oxide used in electrochemical applications. Iridium coordination chemistry includes phosphine, carbonyl, hydride and organometallic compounds. Its resistance to corrosion and ability to support catalytic surface states make iridium important in specialised electrochemistry, but catalytic performance depends strongly on surface structure, oxidation state and electrolyte.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Iridium is used in high-performance spark-plug contacts, crucibles, specialised alloys and components requiring high-temperature corrosion resistance. Platinum–iridium alloys have historical and metrological importance, including the former international metre prototype. Iridium-based electrocatalysts, especially oxide materials, are important in acidic oxygen-evolution environments such as proton-exchange-membrane water electrolysis. Catalyst activity, stability and dissolution must be measured under defined potential, temperature and electrolyte conditions.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Elemental iridium is highly inert and RSC reports low toxicity for the metal. Soluble salts, oxides and organometallic compounds may have different hazard profiles and should be assessed individually. Iridium is extremely rare and is usually recovered as a by-product of nickel refining, so recycling and material-efficiency are important in catalyst and electronics supply chains.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Iridium was discovered with osmium by Smithson Tennant in 1803 from the residue left after aqua-regia treatment of crude platinum. The name derives from the Latin *iris*, meaning rainbow, because of the varied colours of its salts.
