# Element 38: Strontium

**Symbol:** Sr  
**Atomic number:** 38  
**Group / period / block:** 2 / 5 / s  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 87.62(1)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Kr] 5s²  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 5.6949 eV (PDG 2025 review of NIST data)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ⁸⁴Sr, ⁸⁶Sr, ⁸⁷Sr and ⁸⁸Sr  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-geochemistry, radiological and compound-specific materials data require additional review.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Strontium is an alkaline-earth metal with neutral ground configuration [Kr]5s² and ground term ¹S₀. Its dominant oxidation state is +2, forming Sr²⁺. The cation has a strong preference for ionic compounds and oxygen-donor coordination environments. Its chemical behaviour is broadly similar to calcium and barium, with ionic size and solubility trends controlling mineral formation and aqueous speciation.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative amount fractions are ⁸⁴Sr 0.0056(2), ⁸⁶Sr 0.0986(20), ⁸⁷Sr 0.0700(20) and ⁸⁸Sr 0.8258(35). The standard atomic weight is 87.62(1). ⁸⁷Sr is stable and is also produced radiogenically by beta decay of ⁸⁷Rb. The ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr ratio is widely used in geochronology, petrology, archaeology and provenance studies. Radioactive ⁹⁰Sr, a fission product with half-life about 28.8 years, is not part of the stable natural isotope abundance table and requires separate radiological treatment.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar strontium is a soft, silvery metal with a face-centred cubic structure at ambient conditions. The RSC compilation lists melting point 777 °C, boiling point 1382 °C and density 2.64 g cm⁻³. Strontium reacts with air and water and forms an oxide or hydroxide surface layer. Mechanical and chemical properties depend on purity and exposure history.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Sr(II) is the dominant oxidation state. Strontium forms salts such as carbonate, sulfate, nitrate and halides. SrCO₃ is used as a feedstock for other strontium compounds, while SrSO₄ is a sparingly soluble mineral (celestine). Sr²⁺ can substitute for calcium in some minerals and biological systems; isotope and mineral composition influence geochemical interpretation. Solubility and transport depend on pH, carbonate, sulfate, ionic strength and competing cations.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Strontium compounds are used in red pyrotechnic colourants, ferrite magnets, specialty glass and ceramic materials. Strontium titanate (SrTiO₃) is an important dielectric and functional oxide studied in electronics and oxide-interface research. The properties of SrTiO₃ and other compounds depend on crystal defects, stoichiometry and processing; they are not elemental strontium properties. The ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr system is important in geological and archaeological provenance analysis.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Elemental strontium is reactive and should be protected from moisture and air. Stable strontium salts vary in solubility and toxicity; soluble salts can affect calcium metabolism at high exposures. Radioactive ⁹⁰Sr is a significant radiological contaminant because it behaves chemically like calcium and can be incorporated into bone. Assessments must distinguish stable Sr, elemental metal, soluble compounds and ⁹⁰Sr.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Strontium was identified in 1790 by Adair Crawford and later isolated by Humphry Davy in 1808. It was named after Strontian, a village in Scotland where the mineral strontianite was found.
