# Element 36: Krypton

**Symbol:** Kr  
**Atomic number:** 36  
**Group:** 18  
**Period:** 4  
**Block:** p-block  
**Classification:** Noble gas  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** 83.798(2)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Krypton is a period-4 p-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶. The periodic classification is useful for orientation, but chemical properties must be interpreted in terms of electronic structure, phase, oxidation state, and reaction conditions.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
CIAAW lists six naturally occurring isotopes: ⁷⁸Kr, ⁸⁰Kr, ⁸²Kr, ⁸³Kr, ⁸⁴Kr, and ⁸⁶Kr, with representative amount fractions 0.00355(3), 0.02286(10), 0.11593(31), 0.11500(19), 0.56987(15), and 0.17279(41), respectively. ⁷⁸Kr is radioactive through double electron capture with an extraordinarily long half-life; the other five are stable. Trace atmospheric ⁸¹Kr and ⁸⁵Kr are radioactive environmental tracers but do not materially affect standard atomic weight.

## 3. Physical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar krypton is a colourless, odourless noble gas. RSC lists a melting point of 115.78 K, boiling point of 119.735 K, and gas density of 0.003425 g cm⁻³ under its reference conditions. Gas density depends strongly on temperature and pressure, which must be stated in any quantitative comparison.

**Evidence status:** Reference values are taken from the RSC periodic-table fact box. These are secondary reference values, not a critical review of primary measurements. ## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Krypton has a closed-shell 4p⁶ valence configuration and is chemically inert under ordinary conditions. A limited set of compounds is known, especially krypton difluoride, KrF₂, prepared under strongly energetic or specialized conditions. The +2 oxidation state is the best-known formal oxidation state in krypton chemistry; ordinary oxidation-state tables should not imply broad ambient reactivity.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Krypton is used in specialized lighting, photographic flash lamps, insulating glazing, and excimer-laser systems. Krypton-fluoride excimer lasers are important in selected ultraviolet applications. Atmospheric ⁸⁵Kr and cosmogenic ⁸¹Kr are useful tracers in environmental science, groundwater dating, and studies of nuclear releases.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Krypton is chemically inert and not generally chemically toxic, but as a simple asphyxiant it can displace oxygen in confined spaces. Cryogenic liquid krypton presents severe cold-contact and pressure hazards. Radioactive krypton isotopes require isotope-specific radiological assessment.
