# Element 028: Nickel

**Symbol:** Ni  
**Atomic number:** 28  
**Group:** 10  
**Period:** 4  
**Block:** d-block  
**Classification:** Transition metal  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW 2024):** 58.6934(4)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s² [3]  
**Ground-state term symbol:** ³F₄ [3]
**Physical state at approximately 298 K and 1 bar:** Solid [4]

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Nickel is a fourth-period d-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s². Chemical behaviour cannot be inferred from the configuration alone: oxidation state, ligand field, coordination geometry, phase, and bonding environment all influence observed properties. For group 12 zinc, the filled d subshell gives chemistry that differs from the more conventionally redox-active transition metals.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Naturally occurring nickel has five stable isotopes: ⁵⁸Ni, ⁶⁰Ni, ⁶¹Ni, ⁶²Ni, and ⁶⁴Ni. ⁵⁸Ni is the most abundant. The standard atomic weight reflects the isotope-weighted composition of normal terrestrial materials and should not be confused with the mass number of the most abundant isotope.

## 3. Physical properties
At approximately 298 K and 1 bar, nickel is a silvery, corrosion-resistant, ferromagnetic solid. RSC gives a reference melting point of 1728 K, boiling point of 3186 K, and density of 8.90 g cm⁻³. Values depend on purity, phase, and measurement convention; these conditions must accompany tabulated values in a research reference.

**Data status:** Reference values transcribed from the RSC periodic-table fact box. They are suitable for orientation, not yet a substitute for a property-by-property critical review of primary measurements and evaluated handbooks. The final edition must record uncertainty and source-specific measurement conditions wherever available.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
The +2 oxidation state dominates common aqueous and coordination chemistry; +3 and other states occur in selected compounds and strongly oxidizing environments. Nickel forms stable complexes with nitrogen-, oxygen-, sulfur-, and carbon-donor ligands. Its catalytic chemistry includes hydrogenation and cross-coupling transformations, with activity strongly dependent on ligand and oxidation state.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Nickel is a major alloying constituent in stainless steels and corrosion-resistant superalloys. Other important uses include nickel-based superalloys, electroplating, batteries, and catalysts. Distinguish metallic nickel, nickel alloys, and individual nickel compounds when discussing performance or hazards.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Nickel metal and nickel compounds do not share identical toxicological profiles. Certain nickel compounds are established occupational carcinogenic hazards, particularly by inhalation; skin sensitization is also important. Risk statements must identify the chemical species and exposure route rather than generalize from the element alone.
