# Element 90: Thorium

**Symbol:** Th  
**Atomic number:** 90  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 232.0377(4)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 6d² 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ³F₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.30670 eV (NIST ASD 5.12)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ²³⁰Th and ²³²Th  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear-fuel-cycle, decay-chain and material-property datasets require specialist evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Thorium is an actinoid with neutral ground configuration [Rn]6d²7s² and ground term ³F₂. Its dominant oxidation state is +4, producing Th⁴⁺ with a closed-shell radon-like core after removal of four outer electrons. Lower oxidation states have been observed in specialised compounds, but Th(IV) dominates ordinary inorganic and aqueous chemistry. Relativistic effects influence the 5f/6d/7s orbital energies and bonding.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW recognises two naturally occurring thorium isotopes: ²³⁰Th, amount fraction 0.0002(2), and ²³²Th, 0.9998(2). The standard atomic weight is 232.0377(4). ²³²Th is an alpha emitter with half-life 1.40(1)×10¹⁰ years; ²³⁰Th has half-life 7.54(2)×10⁴ years and occurs in the ²³⁸U decay series. The ²³²Th decay chain eventually produces stable ²⁰⁸Pb. The ²³²Th-²⁰⁸Pb system is useful in geochronology. Isotope ratios and decay constants need evaluated uncertainties and sample context.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At ambient conditions thorium is a silvery radioactive metal with a face-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1750 °C, boiling point 4785 °C and density 11.7 g cm⁻³. Thorium metal is relatively ductile and can form a protective oxide layer, though finely divided material may oxidise more readily. Thermophysical data require temperature, pressure, purity and oxygen-content metadata.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Thorium is primarily tetravalent. ThO₂ is a refractory oxide with a very high melting point and strong chemical stability; it has been used in high-temperature ceramics and historically in gas mantles. Thorium(IV) forms halides, nitrates, oxo-species and high-coordinate complexes, often with strong hydrolysis in aqueous solution. Its speciation depends on pH, carbonate, phosphate, fluoride and organic ligands. The solubility and mobility of thorium in soils and waters are strongly mineral- and pH-dependent.

## 5. Materials science and nuclear-energy relevance
Thorium is used in specialised magnesium alloys and research ceramics. ²³²Th is fertile rather than fissile: after neutron capture it can form ²³³Th, which beta decays to ²³³Pa and then to fissile ²³³U. This nuclear-fuel-cycle concept is of interest for reactor research but requires engineered neutron systems, fuel fabrication, reprocessing and safeguards; thorium itself is not directly equivalent to fissile uranium-235. ThO₂ is also studied for refractory and nuclear-material applications.

## 6. Radiological and chemical safety
Thorium is naturally radioactive. External exposure from bulk material may be lower than internal exposure, but inhaled or ingested dust can deliver alpha radiation to tissues. Thorium decay products include radon and other radionuclides, so dust control, containment and waste management are important. Chemical risk depends on compound and solubility. Work with thorium requires radiological protection controls and isotope-specific exposure assessment.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Thorium was discovered in 1828 by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in the mineral thorite. It was named after Thor, the Norse god of thunder. Its discovery preceded the recognition of radioactivity by many decades; thorium later became a key element in the development of nuclear physics and radiochemistry.
