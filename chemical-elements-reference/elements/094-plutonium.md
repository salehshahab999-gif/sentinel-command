# Element 94: Plutonium

**Symbol:** Pu  
**Atomic number:** 94  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [244]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f⁶ 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁷F₀  
**First ionization energy:** 6.0258 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Important isotopes:** ²³⁸Pu, ²³⁹Pu, ²⁴⁰Pu, ²⁴¹Pu, ²⁴²Pu and ²⁴⁴Pu  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; phase, nuclear-decay and radiological data require specialist evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Plutonium is a transuranium actinoid with neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f⁶7s² and ground term ⁷F₀. Its 5f electrons lie near the boundary between localised and itinerant behaviour, contributing to unusually complex electronic, magnetic and structural properties. Plutonium compounds can exhibit oxidation states +3, +4, +5 and +6. The stability of each state depends on ligand, solvent, redox potential and solid-state environment.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Plutonium has no stable isotopes and therefore no standard atomic weight. The bracketed mass number [244] is a representative value, not a standard atomic weight. Important isotopes include ²³⁸Pu (half-life about 87.7 years), ²³⁹Pu (about 24,100 years), ²⁴⁰Pu (about 6,560 years), ²⁴¹Pu (about 14.3 years), ²⁴²Pu (about 3.75×10⁵ years) and ²⁴⁴Pu (about 8.12×10⁷ years). ²³⁸Pu is an alpha emitter used in radioisotope power systems; ²³⁹Pu and other isotopes are relevant to nuclear fuel science. All isotope-specific decay and neutron data require evaluated nuclear references.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
Plutonium metal exhibits several allotropes with markedly different densities, crystal structures and mechanical properties. The RSC compilation lists melting point 640 °C, boiling point 3228 °C and density 19.7 g cm⁻³, but density depends strongly on allotrope and sample composition. Small temperature changes can induce phase transitions and large volume changes. Gallium and other alloy additions can stabilise selected phases, illustrating the complex interplay between electronic structure and lattice behaviour. A single bulk-density value must therefore be qualified by phase and temperature.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Plutonium forms compounds in multiple oxidation states, with Pu(III), Pu(IV), Pu(V) and Pu(VI) important in different systems. Oxides such as PuO₂ are central to materials chemistry. In aqueous solution, Pu can form complex hydrolysed and colloidal species, and redox interconversion can influence solubility and transport. Environmental behaviour depends on oxidation state, pH, carbonate, organic ligands, mineral surfaces and colloid formation. Total plutonium concentration alone is not sufficient to describe mobility.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Plutonium is important in nuclear science, fuel-cycle research and specialised isotope power systems. ²³⁸Pu has been used as a heat source in radioisotope thermoelectric generators for deep-space missions. Plutonium-bearing nuclear fuels require detailed materials, irradiation, safety and safeguards evaluation. Because phase behaviour and radiological properties are complex, materials performance must be tied to isotope composition, alloy, fabrication history and temperature.

## 6. Radiological and chemical safety
Plutonium is highly radiotoxic, especially when inhaled or ingested as fine particles or soluble compounds. Alpha radiation is the principal internal radiation hazard, while chemical toxicity also matters. Different isotopes have distinct decay heat, neutron emissions and dose characteristics. Handling requires licensed nuclear facilities, engineered containment, contamination monitoring, criticality safety and isotope-specific dosimetry. This entry is a scientific overview and intentionally omits operational production, separation or weapon-related procedures.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Plutonium was first produced in 1940 by Glenn Seaborg, Edwin McMillan, Joseph Kennedy and Arthur Wahl at the University of California, Berkeley. It was named after Pluto, the planet then regarded as following Neptune, continuing the planetary naming sequence of uranium and neptunium.

## 8. Evidence quality and unresolved work
- **Atomic weight status:** no standard atomic weight; [244] is a representative mass number only.
- **Atomic structure:** NIST/PDG compilation; detailed ASD level extraction and solid-state electronic structure remain pending.
- **Physical properties:** RSC values are bulk reference values; phase-specific density, thermal and mechanical properties must be specified.
- **Pending:** ENSDF/NuDat isotope-by-isotope decay evaluation; evaluated neutron data; Pu(III-VI) aqueous thermodynamics; phase diagram and alloy constitutive-property datasets; radiation dose and containment guidance.
