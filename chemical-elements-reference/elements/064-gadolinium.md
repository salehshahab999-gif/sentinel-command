# Element 64: Gadolinium

**Symbol:** Gd  
**Atomic number:** 64  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 157.249(2) (revised in 2024)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f⁷ 5d 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁹D°₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.14980 eV (NIST)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁵²Gd, ¹⁵⁴Gd, ¹⁵⁵Gd, ¹⁵⁶Gd, ¹⁵⁷Gd, ¹⁵⁸Gd and ¹⁶⁰Gd  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; detailed nuclear, magnetocaloric and materials datasets require additional evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Gadolinium has the exceptional neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f⁷5d6s². Its half-filled 4f subshell is associated with a high-spin state, while the 5d electron contributes to the observed ⁹D°₂ ground term. The common oxidation state is +3, producing Gd³⁺ with a particularly stable 4f⁷ configuration. Gd²⁺ compounds also exist under suitable solid-state or strongly reducing conditions. Atomic spectroscopy is strongly influenced by open-shell multiplets and spin-orbit interactions.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
The CIAAW 2024 representative amount fractions are: ¹⁵²Gd 0.00204(2), ¹⁵⁴Gd 0.02187(9), ¹⁵⁵Gd 0.14828(60), ¹⁵⁶Gd 0.20493(22), ¹⁵⁷Gd 0.15657(17), ¹⁵⁸Gd 0.24820(20) and ¹⁶⁰Gd 0.21811(28). The current standard atomic weight is 157.249(2). This is an important recent correction: the older 157.25(3) value, still reproduced by some legacy compilations, was superseded after new isotope-composition measurements and the CIAAW 2024 revision. ¹⁵²Gd is an extremely long-lived alpha emitter, with a half-life exceeding 10¹⁴ years; isotope-specific decay properties and uncertainties should be taken from ENSDF/NuDat.

Two isotopes, ¹⁵⁵Gd and ¹⁵⁷Gd, have exceptionally large thermal-neutron capture cross sections. Their importance in neutron physics is isotope-specific and depends on neutron energy and evaluated cross-section data. Natural gadolinium's total capture response must not be confused with the response of an isotopically enriched target.

## 3. Physical and magnetic properties
At ambient pressure, gadolinium metal has a hexagonal close-packed structure. It is ferromagnetic below its Curie temperature, close to room temperature (approximately 293 K), and paramagnetic above it. This proximity to room temperature makes gadolinium a canonical magnetocaloric material. The RSC fact box lists melting point 1313 °C, boiling point 3273 °C and density 7.90 g cm⁻³. Magnetic and thermal measurements require explicit temperature, field, sample purity and crystallographic state. Oxidation and surface contamination can materially affect measured metal properties.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and coordination chemistry
Gd(III) is the predominant oxidation state in aqueous and oxide chemistry. The ion is a hard Lewis acid with high coordination numbers and a preference for oxygen-donor ligands. Gd₂O₃ is a common sesquioxide. In water, hydrolysis, pH and competing ligands govern speciation; free Gd³⁺ should not be treated as equivalent to a stable chelate. The f⁷ configuration gives Gd³⁺ a large magnetic moment and makes it useful in magnetic-resonance applications.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Gadolinium is used in specialty alloys, magnetocaloric refrigeration research, neutron-absorbing materials and phosphors. Gd-containing compounds and alloys have been studied for magnetic cooling because their magnetic entropy changes strongly near magnetic ordering temperatures. Gadolinium-based MRI contrast agents use chelated Gd(III), not unbound aqueous Gd³⁺. Ligand stability, kinetic inertness, renal clearance and the distinction between linear and macrocyclic chelates are central to safety assessments. Gadolinium compounds are also used in scintillators, optical materials and neutron capture systems.

## 6. Toxicology and environmental considerations
Soluble, uncomplexed gadolinium ions can be biologically harmful. Clinical contrast agents are engineered as coordination complexes to reduce the availability of free Gd³⁺, but chelate stability and patient-specific conditions remain relevant to risk assessment. Gadolinium anomalies in aquatic systems have been reported downstream of medical and industrial sources; the environmental behaviour of anthropogenic Gd differs from that of naturally occurring mineral-bound Gd. Assessments must distinguish total gadolinium, dissolved species, particulate phases and chelated forms.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Gadolinium was identified in 1886 by Jean-Charles Galissard de Marignac and named after the mineral gadolinite, itself named for Finnish chemist Johan Gadolin. Its history is part of the nineteenth-century separation of the rare-earth elements, whose chemical similarity made purification exceptionally difficult.
