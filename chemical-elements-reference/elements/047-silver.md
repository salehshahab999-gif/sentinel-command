# Element 47: Silver

**Symbol:** Ag  
**Atomic number:** 47  
**Group / period / block:** 11 / 5 / d  
**Standard atomic weight:** 107.8682(2)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s¹  
**Ground-state term:** ²S₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 7.5762 eV  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid  
**Evidence status:** Initial research draft; numerical values below are source-attributed, not a completed critical evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and quantum properties
Silver has a closed 4d subshell and one 5s valence electron. The ground configuration and term are tabulated in the NIST periodic-table compilation; the quoted first ionization energy is likewise a compilation value. A full spectroscopic treatment requires level-by-level extraction from NIST ASD, including level uncertainties, transition probabilities, wavelengths, isotope shifts, and hyperfine structure. Do not treat the compact periodic-table entry as a substitute for an ASD line-list audit.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopic composition
CIAAW lists two stable naturally occurring nuclides: **¹⁰⁷Ag**, atomic mass 106.90509(2) Da and amount fraction 0.51839(8); **¹⁰⁹Ag**, 108.904756(9) Da and 0.48161(8). The standard atomic weight is 107.8682(2). The abundance values are evaluated representative terrestrial values, not universal constants for every sample. Nuclear spin, magnetic moments, evaluated level schemes, and capture data require isotope-specific ENSDF/NNDC and evaluated nuclear-data retrieval.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar silver is a solid metal with a face-centred cubic (fcc) structure under ordinary conditions. The RSC periodic-table compilation gives melting point 961.78 °C (1234.93 K), boiling point 2162 °C (2435 K), and density 10.5 g cm⁻³. These are reference-condition values; a final edition must attach provenance, uncertainty, purity, and measurement conditions to each property. Silver is notable for very high electrical and thermal conductivity, but precise values depend on temperature, purity, microstructure, and measurement method and are intentionally not asserted here without a primary or critically evaluated source.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The dominant oxidation state in ordinary inorganic chemistry is +1. Ag(I) forms linear, tetrahedral, and higher-coordinate complexes depending on ligand and medium; common ligands include halides, ammonia, thiosulfate, and cyanide. AgCl, AgBr, and AgI are sparingly soluble and photosensitive to differing degrees. Silver metal tarnishes through surface reaction with reduced-sulfur species, producing dark Ag₂S; this is not simply oxidation by dry oxygen. Ag(II) and higher formal oxidation states occur in specialised compounds and strongly oxidising environments, but are much less representative of bulk silver chemistry.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Applications include electrical contacts, conductive pastes, brazing alloys, mirrors and optical coatings, antimicrobial surfaces, jewellery, and photographic chemistry. Contact performance depends on sulfide tarnish, contact force, environment, and alloying. Silver-copper alloys are used where mechanical strength exceeds that of pure silver. Nanostructured silver requires separate treatment because particle size, surface ligands, oxidation/dissolution, and aggregation alter its optical and antimicrobial behaviour.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
Bulk metallic silver has relatively low acute toxicity by ordinary handling routes, but chronic exposure to silver compounds or particles can cause argyria, a persistent blue-grey discoloration. Soluble silver salts can irritate and damage tissues; environmental release is relevant because bioavailable silver is toxic to aquatic organisms. Cyanide-containing silver processes carry severe independent cyanide hazards. Exposure assessment must distinguish metal, soluble salts, nanoparticles, and complexed silver.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Silver has been known since prehistoric times. The symbol Ag derives from Latin *argentum*. The English name is historically derived from Germanic forms. Its long role in coinage, standards, and electrochemistry has made silver important in the historical development of quantitative chemistry.
