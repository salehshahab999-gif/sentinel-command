# Element 029: Copper

**Symbol:** Cu  
**Atomic number:** 29  
**Group:** 11  
**Period:** 4  
**Block:** d-block  
**Classification:** Transition metal  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** 63.546(3)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹  
**Physical state at approximately 298 K and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Copper is a fourth-period d-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. Chemical behaviour cannot be inferred from the configuration alone: oxidation state, ligand field, coordination geometry, phase, and bonding environment all influence observed properties. For group 12 zinc, the filled d subshell gives chemistry that differs from the more conventionally redox-active transition metals.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Natural copper consists primarily of two stable isotopes, ⁶³Cu and ⁶⁵Cu. CIAAW reports atomic masses and amount fractions of 62.929 597(3) Da and 0.6915(15) for ⁶³Cu, and 64.927 790(5) Da and 0.3085(15) for ⁶⁵Cu. Natural isotopic variability is relevant to the stated standard atomic-weight uncertainty.

## 3. Physical properties
At approximately 298 K and 1 bar, copper is a reddish, ductile, face-centred-cubic metal. RSC gives a reference melting point of 1357.77 K, boiling point of 2833 K, and density of 8.96 g cm⁻³. Its exceptionally high electrical and thermal conductivity make purity, defects, and microstructure important in materials applications.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Copper commonly exhibits +1 and +2 oxidation states. Cu(I) is d¹⁰ and often favours low-coordinate or soft-donor environments, whereas Cu(II) is d⁹ and frequently exhibits Jahn–Teller distortion in coordination complexes. Copper participates in redox cycling, oxide and sulfide mineral chemistry, and biologically important metalloenzyme active sites.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Major applications include electrical wiring, motors, heat exchangers, plumbing, electronics, and alloys such as bronze and brass. Copper's conductivity is sensitive to impurities and defects; alloying improves selected mechanical properties at the cost of electrical conductivity.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Copper is an essential trace element, but excessive exposure to soluble copper compounds can cause adverse effects. Environmental risk depends on speciation, bioavailability, and water chemistry. Dusts and fumes generated in industrial processing require appropriate controls.
