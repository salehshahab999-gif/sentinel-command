# Element 49: Indium

**Symbol:** In  
**Atomic number:** 49  
**Group / period / block:** 13 / 5 / p  
**Standard atomic weight:** 114.818(1)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s² 5p¹  
**Ground-state term:** ²P°₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 5.7864 eV  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid  
**Evidence status:** Initial research draft; numerical claims remain subject to source-level audit.

## 1. Atomic and quantum properties
Indium is a period-5 p-block element with a filled 4d shell and a 5s²5p¹ valence configuration. Its tabulated ground term is ²P°₁/₂. The first ionization energy and compact term assignment are from the NIST periodic-table compilation; current NIST ASD extraction is required for a final spectroscopic account, including fine-structure splitting, level uncertainties, hyperfine constants, and isotope shifts.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopic composition
CIAAW standard atomic weight is 114.818(1). Natural indium consists primarily of ¹¹⁵In, amount fraction 0.95719(52), and ¹¹³In, 0.04281(52). ¹¹⁵In is extremely long-lived radioactive, not strictly stable; ¹¹³In is also radioactive with a very long half-life. The natural abundance table is an evaluated composition, not evidence that both nuclides are stable. Half-lives, decay branches, spins, and nuclear levels must be quoted from a current ENSDF evaluation, with evaluation date and uncertainty.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
Indium is a soft, silvery metal and is solid at 20 °C and 1 bar. Its ambient crystal structure is body-centred tetragonal. PubChem's periodic-table compilation lists a melting point of 429.75 K. The low melting temperature, softness, and ability to wet many surfaces underpin its use in solders and thermal-interface materials. Quantitative mechanical, thermal, and thermodynamic properties must be tied to purity, texture, temperature, and measurement protocol.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The +3 oxidation state is the principal state in ordinary indium chemistry; +1 compounds are also established and can be stabilised by appropriate ligands and solid-state environments. Indium(III) forms halides, oxides, chalcogenides, and coordination complexes. Indium oxide and indium tin oxide (ITO) are important transparent conducting materials. Indium chemistry in aqueous media depends strongly on pH, hydrolysis, and ligand complexation; simple oxidation-state labels alone do not specify solution speciation.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Major applications include ITO transparent electrodes, low-temperature solders, semiconductor compounds such as InP and InAs, compound-semiconductor epitaxy, and thermal-interface alloys. ITO performance depends on oxygen stoichiometry, carrier concentration, dopant distribution, microstructure, and deposition conditions. Indium's supply chain is linked largely to zinc processing, making recovery from manufacturing scrap and end-of-life products an important materials-economics issue.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
Indium metal and its compounds should not be treated as toxicologically interchangeable. Inhalation of indium-containing dusts, especially certain poorly soluble compounds and occupational process aerosols, has been associated with serious pulmonary disease. Indium phosphide has specific carcinogenicity concerns in hazard assessments. Risk statements must identify the chemical species and exposure route; avoid generalising a single compound's classification to all indium materials.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Indium was discovered spectroscopically in 1863 by Ferdinand Reich and Hieronymous Theodor Richter. Its name refers to the indigo-blue spectral line that helped identify it. The discovery is a landmark example of spectroscopy enabling identification of a previously unknown element.
