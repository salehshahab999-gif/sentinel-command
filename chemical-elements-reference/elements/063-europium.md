# Element 63: Europium

**Symbol:** Eu  
**Atomic number:** 63  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 151.964(1)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f⁷ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁸S°₇/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 5.670385 eV (NIST)  
**Naturally occurring nuclides:** ¹⁵¹Eu and ¹⁵³Eu  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; detailed spectroscopic and nuclear-data extraction remains to be completed.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Europium is a lanthanoid whose neutral atom has a half-filled 4f shell, [Xe]4f⁷6s². The high-spin 4f⁷ arrangement gives the ⁸S°₇/₂ ground term. Europium is unusual among the lanthanoids because both +2 and +3 oxidation states are chemically important. Eu²⁺ retains a 4f⁷ configuration, while Eu³⁺ is 4f⁶; redox stability depends on the ligand, lattice, solvent and oxygen chemical potential. Its low first ionization energy is consistent with the relatively high reactivity of the metal.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW lists two naturally occurring isotopes: ¹⁵¹Eu, amount fraction 0.4781(6), and ¹⁵³Eu, 0.5219(6). Their evaluated atomic masses are 150.919857(9) Da and 152.921237(9) Da, respectively. CIAAW gives 151.964(1) as the standard atomic weight. ¹⁵¹Eu is an extremely long-lived alpha emitter (the commonly cited half-life is on the order of 10¹⁸ years), so its frequent description as stable is a practical convention rather than a statement of absolute nuclear stability. Precise half-life, decay energy and branching data must be taken from the current ENSDF/NuDat evaluation. Both stable-abundance fractions and radioactive decay data require explicit source versioning.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar europium is a solid, soft, silvery metal. It has a body-centred cubic structure at ambient conditions and is notably less dense and has a much lower melting point than most other lanthanoids. The Royal Society of Chemistry (RSC) compilation lists a melting point of 822 °C, a boiling point of 1529 °C and a density of 5.24 g cm⁻³. These are reference values, not a substitute for a full thermodynamic dataset; pressure, purity, oxidation and phase history should be reported for precision work.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and compounds
The common oxidation states are +2 and +3. Eu(II) salts are often strong reducing agents in aqueous chemistry, whereas Eu(III) is prevalent in many oxygen-donor complexes and oxides. Europium oxides include EuO (divalent) and Eu₂O₃ (trivalent); mixed-valence materials require separate electronic-structure treatment. Coordination number, hydration and redox potential depend strongly on solvent and ligand environment. A tabulated oxidation state must not be mistaken for a complete solution-speciation model.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Europium ions are important luminescent activators. Eu³⁺ commonly produces narrow red emission, while Eu²⁺ emission is often broad and host-dependent, frequently blue to green. These properties underpin phosphors for displays, lighting and optical materials. Europium compounds are also used in security inks and anti-counterfeiting features; the red luminescence under ultraviolet excitation has been used in euro-banknote security. The emission spectrum depends on oxidation state, host lattice, concentration, defects and excitation conditions, so data should identify the exact compound and matrix.

## 6. Safety and environmental notes
Bulk europium metal is reactive and should be protected from air and moisture; finely divided material can present enhanced fire risk. Toxicological information for europium compounds is less extensive than for common industrial metals. Avoid describing all Eu compounds as harmless: exposure route, solubility, particle form and ligand chemistry matter. Rare-earth extraction and phosphor recycling also raise occupational and environmental management issues.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Europium was identified by Eugène-Anatole Demarçay in the late nineteenth century and named after Europe. Its separation from samarium-containing material illustrates the analytical difficulty of resolving chemically similar lanthanoids.
