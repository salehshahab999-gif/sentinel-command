# Element 74: Tungsten

**Symbol:** W  
**Atomic number:** 74  
**Group / period / block:** 6 / 6 / d  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 183.84(1)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d⁴ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁵D₀  
**First ionization energy:** 7.86403 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁸⁰W, ¹⁸²W, ¹⁸³W, ¹⁸⁴W and ¹⁸⁶W  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific nuclear, high-temperature and industrial-material datasets require further evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Tungsten is a group-6 transition metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d⁴6s² and ground term ⁵D₀. Its chemistry spans oxidation states from low-valent compounds to the common +6 state. Tungsten(VI) oxo-species, tungstates and tungsten oxides are important in aqueous, solid-state and catalytic chemistry. The element's high cohesive energy and refractory behaviour make it important in high-temperature engineering.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative amount fractions are: ¹⁸⁰W 0.0012(1), ¹⁸²W 0.2650(16), ¹⁸³W 0.1431(4), ¹⁸⁴W 0.3064(2) and ¹⁸⁶W 0.2843(19). The standard atomic weight is 183.84(1). Several naturally occurring tungsten nuclides are extremely long-lived alpha emitters; for example ¹⁸⁰W has a half-life on the order of 10¹⁸ years. ¹⁸³W has nuclear spin I = 1/2, while the even-mass isotopes have zero ground-state spin. Isotope-specific decay properties and neutron-capture data should be taken from evaluated nuclear datasets.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At ambient conditions tungsten has a body-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists a melting point of 3414 °C, boiling point of 5555 °C and density of 19.3 g cm⁻³. Its melting point is the highest among the metals at ordinary pressure. Tungsten retains useful strength at elevated temperature, but oxidation in air becomes significant at high temperatures. Thermal conductivity, creep and recrystallisation depend on purity, grain structure, alloy additions and manufacturing history.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Tungsten(VI) is common in oxides and oxyanions such as tungstate, WO₄²⁻. Tungsten also forms lower-valent halides, oxides, sulfides and organometallic compounds. Tungsten carbide (WC) is an extremely hard ceramic-metal material used in cutting and wear applications; its hardness and fracture behaviour are compound and microstructure properties, not intrinsic properties of elemental W. In aqueous chemistry, tungsten speciation depends on pH, concentration, ionic strength and polymerisation state.

## 5. Materials science and industrial applications
Tungsten is used in electrodes, heating elements, field emitters, high-temperature furnace components, X-ray targets and high-speed tool steels. Tungsten carbide composites are used for machining and wear-resistant tooling. Dense tungsten alloys are used for counterweights, radiation shielding and specialised engineering components. Modern applications also include plasma-facing and fusion-research components, where sputtering, recrystallisation, neutron damage and transmutation must be evaluated. The former widespread use in incandescent-lamp filaments has declined in many markets but remains historically important.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
The toxicology of tungsten depends on chemical form and exposure route. Soluble tungsten compounds, insoluble oxides and hard-metal dusts should not be treated as equivalent. Cobalt-containing tungsten-carbide hard metals can present occupational risks distinct from pure tungsten. Mining, powder metallurgy and machining require dust controls and management of metal-bearing waste.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Tungsten was identified in the late eighteenth century. The symbol W derives from *wolfram*, while the name tungsten derives from Swedish *tung sten*, meaning heavy stone. The element's discovery and naming reflect its occurrence in minerals that interfered with tin smelting.
