# Element 86: Radon

**Symbol:** Rn  
**Atomic number:** 86  
**Group / period / block:** 18 / 6 / p  
**Standard atomic weight:** none (no stable isotopes); representative mass number [222]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s² 6p⁶  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 10.7485 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Representative natural radionuclide:** ²²²Rn  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Gas  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific decay, environmental transport and exposure datasets require further evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Radon is a closed-shell noble gas with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s²6p⁶ and ground term ¹S₀. Its first ionization energy is high, consistent with a filled valence shell. Radon is chemically inert under ordinary conditions, although a limited number of radon compounds have been investigated under specialised conditions. Relativistic effects are significant for this heavy noble-gas atom.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Radon has no stable isotopes and therefore no standard atomic weight. The bracketed mass number [222] is a conventional representative value. Important isotopes include ²²²Rn (half-life 3.8235 days), ²²⁰Rn or thoron (about 55.6 seconds) and ²¹⁹Rn or actinon (about 3.96 seconds). ²²²Rn is generated in the ²³⁸U decay series via radium-226; ²²⁰Rn belongs to the thorium decay series. Radon decays by alpha emission to radioactive progeny, whose subsequent decays contribute significantly to inhalation dose. Isotope-specific nuclear data must be taken from ENSDF/NuDat.

## 3. Physical properties and phase behaviour
At 20 °C and 1 bar radon is a colourless, odourless radioactive gas. The RSC compilation lists melting point −71 °C, boiling point −61.7 °C and gas density 0.009074 g cm⁻³. These are reference values for the element; actual gas density depends on temperature and pressure. Radon is soluble to a limited extent in water and more readily partitions into some organic phases; soil permeability, pressure gradients and ventilation govern its movement into buildings.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Radon has a closed-shell electronic structure and is the least reactive of the common naturally occurring noble gases. Chemical compounds are difficult to prepare and study because radon isotopes decay rapidly and only trace quantities are generally available. Any reported bonding or compound property must specify isotope, matrix, temperature and experimental conditions. For environmental risk, its radioactive decay chain is generally more important than ordinary chemical reactivity.

## 5. Environmental transport and public-health relevance
Radon forms naturally from the radioactive decay of uranium and thorium in rocks and soils. It can migrate through cracks and porous ground and accumulate in buildings, especially in basements and poorly ventilated spaces. When inhaled, radon progeny can deposit on the respiratory tract and emit alpha radiation that damages lung tissue. The World Health Organization identifies radon as a major cause of lung cancer; risk is higher for smokers because of interaction between radon exposure and tobacco smoke. Indoor concentrations vary substantially by geology, construction and ventilation. Measurement with an appropriate radon detector is the basis for assessing exposure.

## 6. Safety and exposure control
Radon is a radiological inhalation hazard, not a conventional chemical-poisoning hazard. Risk is determined by concentration, duration, aerosol attachment and progeny equilibrium. Long-term indoor exposure should be assessed using recognised measurement protocols and local action levels. Building measures can include sealing entry pathways, sub-slab depressurisation and improved ventilation where appropriate. A gas that is colourless and odourless cannot be detected reliably by human senses.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Radon was identified in the early twentieth century through studies of radioactive gases released from radium compounds. It was initially called radium emanation and later named radon. Its name reflects its relationship to radium in the uranium decay chain.

## 8. Evidence quality and unresolved work
- **Atomic weight status:** no standard atomic weight; [222] is a representative mass number only.
- **Atomic configuration and term:** NIST/PDG compilation; detailed excited-state and compound data remain sparse.
- **Physical properties:** RSC compilation; gas density must always be stated at a defined temperature and pressure.
- **Pending:** evaluated decay data for ²²²Rn, ²²⁰Rn and ²¹⁹Rn with daughter progeny; soil-gas transport and indoor accumulation models; building-specific exposure assessment and mitigation performance data.
