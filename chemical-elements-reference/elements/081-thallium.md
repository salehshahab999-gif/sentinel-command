# Element 81: Thallium

**Symbol:** Tl  
**Atomic number:** 81  
**Group / period / block:** 13 / 6 / p  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** [204.382, 204.385]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s² 6p  
**Ground-state term:** ²P°₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.1083 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ²⁰³Tl and ²⁰⁵Tl  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-abundance interval, toxicology and compound-specific applications require careful review.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Thallium is a heavy group-13 p-block element with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s²6p and ground term ²P°₁/₂. The inert-pair effect stabilises the +1 oxidation state relative to +3, especially in many ionic compounds. Tl(I) compounds are common, while Tl(III) compounds are stronger oxidants and may be reduced to Tl(I). Relativistic effects and the stabilisation of the 6s² pair contribute to this unusual oxidation-state preference.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
Natural thallium consists of ²⁰³Tl and ²⁰⁵Tl. CIAAW gives representative amount-fraction ranges of [0.2944, 0.2959] for ²⁰³Tl and [0.7041, 0.7056] for ²⁰⁵Tl, leading to the interval standard atomic weight [204.382, 204.385]. The interval is deliberate: normal terrestrial materials can have measurable isotopic-composition variation, so a single overly precise atomic-weight value is not justified. Both nuclides have non-zero nuclear spin and are relevant to nuclear magnetic resonance and isotope-ratio analysis.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar thallium is a soft, silvery-white solid metal that tarnishes in air. The RSC compilation lists melting point 304 °C, boiling point 1473 °C and density 11.8 g cm⁻³. Its low melting point and softness have supported limited specialised alloy and glass applications, but use is constrained by toxicity. Physical values depend on purity, oxide condition and measurement protocol.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Thallium chemistry is dominated by Tl(I) and Tl(III). Tl(I) resembles alkali-metal cations in some coordination and solubility trends, while Tl(III) has greater oxidising power and stronger complexation with suitable ligands. Thallium forms halides, oxides, sulfides and coordination compounds. Aqueous speciation depends on oxidation state, pH, redox potential and ligand concentration.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Thallium compounds have been used in photoelectric cells, infrared detector materials, high-refractive-index optical glass and low-melting specialty glass. Thallium-201 is used in nuclear-medicine myocardial perfusion imaging under regulated medical protocols. These applications are specific to the compound or radioisotope and should not be conflated with elemental thallium. Many former uses, particularly pesticides and household products, have been restricted or prohibited because of toxicity.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
Thallium is highly toxic. Tl⁺ can mimic potassium in biological systems and disrupt cellular transport, particularly affecting the nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, cardiovascular system and hair follicles. Both acute and chronic exposures can be severe. Soluble thallium salts and dusts require strict exposure prevention and regulated waste handling. This entry intentionally does not treat thallium as an ordinary handling metal; compound solubility and exposure route are critical.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Thallium was discovered spectroscopically by William Crookes in 1861, identified by a bright green spectral line. Its name derives from Greek *thallos*, meaning a green shoot or twig. Claude-Auguste Lamy independently isolated the metal soon after.
