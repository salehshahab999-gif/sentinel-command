# Element 113: Nihonium

**Symbol:** Nh  
**Atomic number:** 113  
**Group / period / block:** 13 / 7 / p  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [286]  
**Predicted ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 6d¹⁰ 7s² 7p  
**Ground-state term:** ²P°₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** not experimentally established; theoretical estimates remain model-dependent  
**Representative isotopes:** ²⁸⁵Nh and ²⁸⁶Nh  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; direct chemical characterisation and all bulk physical properties remain very limited or unknown.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Nihonium is a transactinoid p-block element in group 13, below thallium. Its predicted ground configuration is [Rn]5f¹⁴6d¹⁰7s²7p, with term ²P°₁/₂. The inert-pair effect is expected to stabilise the 7s² electrons and influence the relative stability of +1 and +3 oxidation states. Relativistic effects are particularly important in this heavy p-block element.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Nihonium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight. [286] is a representative mass number. CIAAW/NUBASE2020 list ²⁸⁵Nh with half-life 4.6(1.1) seconds and ²⁸⁶Nh with 12(5) seconds. These values are based on very small event counts; decay-chain correlations, branching and uncertainties should be taken from the latest ENSDF/NuDat and NUBASE records.

## 3. Physical properties and measurement limits
No macroscopic nihonium sample has been produced. RSC lists melting point, boiling point and density as unknown. It is represented as a solid in periodic-table displays, but the bulk state cannot be directly tested. Any predictions of metallic character or phase stability must be labelled theoretical.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Nihonium is expected to resemble thallium in group-13 chemistry, with +1 and +3 oxidation states potentially relevant. The inert-pair effect and relativistic stabilization of the 7s² pair may make Nh(I) more important than a simple extrapolation from lighter group members suggests. No robust direct chemical characterisation is available; oxidation-state assignments remain theoretical predictions.

## 5. Research relevance
Nihonium has no commercial or technological application. Its importance lies in heavy p-block chemistry, relativistic effects and the experimental confirmation of periodic trends at high atomic number. Its discovery demonstrated the capability of accelerator facilities to produce and identify new elements through decay chains.

## 6. Radiological safety
Nihonium isotopes are radioactive and extremely short-lived. Experimental work is limited to licensed facilities with specialised target systems, recoil separators, containment and decay-detection arrays. No conventional handling or bulk exposure data exist.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Nihonium was first synthesised by the RIKEN team in Japan, with the first recognised discovery evidence reported in 2004 and additional confirmation in subsequent experiments. IUPAC approved the name nihonium in 2016. The name derives from *Nihon*, one Japanese name for Japan, meaning “Land of the Rising Sun.”

## 8. Evidence quality and unresolved work
- **Atomic structure:** PDG/NIST compilations provide a predicted configuration and ground term; ionisation energy is not established experimentally.
- **Nuclear data:** CIAAW/NUBASE2020 lists ²⁸⁵Nh and ²⁸⁶Nh; current ENSDF required for detailed decay schemes.
- **Physical and chemical properties:** RSC lists bulk properties as unknown; chemical behaviour remains largely theoretical.
- **Pending:** atom-at-a-time chemical tests; oxidation-state and volatility measurements; improved isotope production and decay data; relativistic electronic-structure calculations with uncertainty.
