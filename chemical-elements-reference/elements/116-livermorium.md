# Element 116: Livermorium

**Symbol:** Lv  
**Atomic number:** 116  
**Group / period / block:** 16 / 7 / p  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [293]  
**Predicted ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 6d¹⁰ 7s² 7p⁴  
**Ground-state term:** ³P₂  
**First ionization energy:** not experimentally established; theoretical estimates are model-dependent  
**Representative isotopes:** ²⁹¹Lv, ²⁹²Lv and ²⁹³Lv  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; nuclear data are available, while bulk physical and chemical properties remain unknown or theoretical.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Livermorium is a transactinoid p-block element in group 16, below polonium. Its predicted neutral ground configuration is [Rn]5f¹⁴6d¹⁰7s²7p⁴, with ground term ³P₂. The inert-pair effect and strong relativistic stabilisation of the 7s² electrons are expected to affect its oxidation states and bonding. Predicted oxidation states and compound stability remain theoretical because no chemical experiment has yet established a robust bulk chemistry.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Livermorium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight. [293] is a representative mass number. CIAAW/NUBASE2020 identify ²⁹³Lv as the longest-lived isotope in the current table, with half-life 70(30) milliseconds. Other measured isotopes include ²⁹¹Lv (26(12) ms) and ²⁹²Lv (16(6) ms). These values are short and uncertainties are large relative to the half-lives; current ENSDF/NuDat evaluations should be consulted for decay modes and daughter chains.

## 3. Physical properties and measurement limits
No macroscopic sample of livermorium has been produced. RSC lists melting point, boiling point and density as unknown. The solid state shown in periodic-table displays is a prediction, not an experimental observation. Bulk crystal structure, thermal conductivity, mechanical strength and phase transitions remain undetermined.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Livermorium is expected to resemble the chalcogens but with a strong inert-pair effect and substantial relativistic corrections. Formal oxidation states such as +2 and +4 may be more stable than a simple extrapolation from lighter chalcogens would suggest, but this remains theoretical. No validated atom-at-a-time chemical characterisation has established its oxidation-state or compound behaviour.

## 5. Research relevance
Livermorium has no commercial or technological uses. Its value is in superheavy-element nuclear physics, relativistic electronic-structure theory and tests of periodic trends in group 16. It is produced in specialised accelerator experiments and identified through radioactive decay chains.

## 6. Radiological safety
Livermorium isotopes are highly radioactive and have millisecond-scale half-lives. Research requires licensed accelerator facilities, target and separator contamination control and isotope-specific radiological procedures. No conventional sample handling or occupational exposure datasets exist.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Livermorium was first observed in 2000 by a collaboration at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, using target material supplied by Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. IUPAC approved the name in 2012, honouring the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.

## 8. Evidence quality and unresolved work
- **Atomic configuration and term:** RSC and PDG compilations; detailed experimental atomic levels and ionization data are unavailable.
- **Nuclear properties:** CIAAW/NUBASE2020 lists half-lives for ²⁹¹–²⁹³Lv; current ENSDF should control decay branches.
- **Physical properties:** melting point, boiling point and density remain unknown experimentally.
- **Pending:** atom-at-a-time chemical investigation; theoretical oxidation-state and phase calculations; improved ²⁹³Lv half-life and decay-chain data.
