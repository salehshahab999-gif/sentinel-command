# Element 67: Holmium

**Symbol:** Ho  
**Atomic number:** 67  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 164.930 329(5)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹¹ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁴I°₁₅/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.0215 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring nuclide:** ¹⁶⁵Ho (monoisotopic)  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; detailed spectroscopy, nuclear and magnetic phase data remain to be audited.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Holmium is a heavy lanthanoid with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹¹6s² and ground term ⁴I°₁₅/₂. Its partially filled 4f shell produces a large magnetic moment and a complex multiplet spectrum. The dominant oxidation state is +3, giving Ho³⁺ with a 4f¹⁰ configuration. The +2 state is much less common and is stabilised only in selected solid-state or strongly reducing environments. Accurate optical and magnetic modelling requires crystal-field splitting and spin-orbit coupling, not just the free-ion term symbol.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear properties
Natural holmium is effectively monoisotopic: ¹⁶⁵Ho has representative amount fraction 1 and is the sole naturally occurring stable isotope. CIAAW gives 164.930329(5) as the standard atomic weight. The isotope has nuclear spin I = 7/2, making hyperfine structure relevant to high-resolution spectroscopy and precision measurements. Radioactive holmium nuclides are produced in nuclear reactions; their decay schemes, half-lives and emission probabilities must be taken from current ENSDF/NuDat evaluations.

## 3. Physical and magnetic properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar holmium is a solid, bright silvery metal with a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1472 °C, boiling point 2700 °C and density 8.80 g cm⁻³. Holmium exhibits strong temperature-dependent magnetic ordering and one of the largest magnetic moments among the elements. Magnetic data require temperature, field, crystal orientation and purity to be specified; the response of a holmium-containing alloy or compound must not be assigned to elemental Ho.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Ho(III) is the overwhelmingly dominant oxidation state in common oxides, halides and aqueous coordination chemistry. Ho₂O₃ is a representative sesquioxide. Ho³⁺ behaves as a hard Lewis acid and favours oxygen-donor ligands, commonly forming high-coordinate complexes. Hydrolysis and speciation depend on pH, ligand concentration and ionic strength. Because adjacent lanthanoids have similar chemistry, separation relies on small differences in ionic radius and complex stability.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Holmium has a high neutron-absorption capacity and is used in specialised neutron-control applications. Holmium-containing alloys and compounds are used in magnetic materials. Ho:YAG lasers emit near 2.1 μm and are used in medical procedures, including applications where water absorption supports precise tissue interaction. The wavelength, pulse regime and biological effect belong to the doped crystal and laser system, not to elemental holmium itself.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Holmium has no established biological role. Bulk metal is reactive and fine powders require appropriate control; compound toxicity depends on chemical form, solubility and exposure route. The absence of a known biological function should not be interpreted as proof of zero hazard. Rare-earth extraction and processing also require management of acidic, saline and particulate waste streams.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Holmium was identified in the nineteenth century during the chemical separation of rare-earth minerals. Its name derives from *Holmia*, the Latin name for Stockholm. The element's discovery illustrates the analytical challenges involved in separating lanthanoids with closely similar chemical properties.
