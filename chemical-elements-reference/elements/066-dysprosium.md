# Element 66: Dysprosium

**Symbol:** Dy  
**Atomic number:** 66  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 162.500(1)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁰ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁵I₈  
**First ionization energy:** 5.939061 eV (NIST current element-data index)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁵⁶Dy, ¹⁵⁸Dy, ¹⁶⁰Dy, ¹⁶¹Dy, ¹⁶²Dy, ¹⁶³Dy and ¹⁶⁴Dy  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; detailed nuclear, magnetic and materials datasets remain open.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Dysprosium is a heavy lanthanoid with a neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁰6s² and ground term ⁵I₈. The open 4f shell produces a substantial magnetic moment and a complex spectrum. In common compounds the oxidation state is +3, giving Dy³⁺ with a 4f⁹ configuration. Divalent dysprosium is known in selected compounds but is much less representative of ordinary oxide, halide and aqueous chemistry. Fine-structure and multiplet assignments should be taken from the current NIST Atomic Spectra Database rather than reconstructed from a single ground-term label.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative isotopic amount fractions are: ¹⁵⁶Dy 0.00056(3), ¹⁵⁸Dy 0.00095(3), ¹⁶⁰Dy 0.02329(18), ¹⁶¹Dy 0.18889(42), ¹⁶²Dy 0.25475(36), ¹⁶³Dy 0.24896(42) and ¹⁶⁴Dy 0.28260(54). The standard atomic weight is 162.500(1). The natural-abundance list includes extremely long-lived radioactive nuclides: ¹⁵⁶Dy and ¹⁵⁸Dy undergo alpha decay on timescales far beyond ordinary laboratory observation. Exact half-lives and decay energies should be cited from evaluated nuclear datasets. ¹⁶¹Dy and ¹⁶³Dy have non-zero nuclear spin and are relevant to hyperfine and nuclear spectroscopy.

## 3. Physical and magnetic properties
At ambient conditions dysprosium is a solid metal with a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1412 °C, boiling point 2567 °C and density 8.55 g cm⁻³. Dysprosium has unusually rich low-temperature magnetic behaviour, including transitions between helicoidal antiferromagnetic and ferromagnetic states. Reported transition temperatures are sensitive to purity, strain, field and sample history; they must be drawn from a phase-specific primary dataset before being used as reference constants. Its strong magnetocrystalline anisotropy is valuable in high-performance magnet alloys.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The +3 oxidation state dominates ordinary dysprosium chemistry. Dy₂O₃ is a common sesquioxide; halides, chalcogenides and coordination complexes are also known. Dy³⁺ is a hard Lewis acid and commonly forms high-coordinate complexes with oxygen-donor ligands. Hydrolysis and aqueous speciation depend on pH, ionic strength and complexing ligands. As with other lanthanoids, chemical separation from neighbouring elements requires exploiting small differences in ionic radius and complex stability.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Dysprosium is important as an alloying addition to Nd-Fe-B permanent magnets. It can improve resistance to thermal demagnetisation and coercivity, which is valuable in motors and generators operating at elevated temperatures. The performance gain depends on where Dy is located in the magnet microstructure; grain-boundary diffusion strategies can reduce the amount required compared with uniform bulk substitution. Dysprosium compounds are also used in specialised lighting and optical materials. Dysprosium oxide-nickel cermet has been studied for neutron-absorbing reactor control applications because of its neutron-capture characteristics and dimensional stability under irradiation.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Dysprosium has no known biological role. The pure metal reacts with air and water, and powders can present greater reactivity than bulk pieces. Toxicity depends on compound form and exposure; limited biological evidence should not be treated as proof of no hazard. Mining and separation of heavy rare earths can generate process wastes requiring controlled management.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Dysprosium was named from the Greek *dysprositos*, meaning difficult to obtain, reflecting the difficulty of separating it from other rare-earth elements. Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran identified it in 1886; Georges Urbain later isolated the metal in 1906.
