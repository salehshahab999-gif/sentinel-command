# Element 027: Cobalt

**Symbol:** Co  
**Atomic number:** 27  
**Group:** 9  
**Period:** 4  
**Block:** d-block  
**Classification:** Transition metal  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** 58.933 194(3)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s²  
**Physical state at approximately 298 K and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Cobalt is a fourth-period d-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s². Chemical behaviour cannot be inferred from the configuration alone: oxidation state, ligand field, coordination geometry, phase, and bonding environment all influence observed properties. For group 12 zinc, the filled d subshell gives chemistry that differs from the more conventionally redox-active transition metals.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Naturally occurring cobalt is monoisotopic: ⁵⁹Co has an isotopic amount fraction of 1. The radionuclide ⁶⁰Co is produced by neutron activation and decays by beta-minus emission to ⁶⁰Ni, with a half-life of approximately 5.27 years.

## 3. Physical properties
At approximately 298 K and 1 bar, cobalt is a solid, lustrous, silvery-blue ferromagnetic metal. RSC tabulates a melting point of 1768 K, boiling point of 3200 K, and density of 8.86 g cm⁻³; these handbook values should be treated as reference values whose sample purity and measurement conventions require checking before final publication.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
The principal oxidation states in ordinary chemistry are +2 and +3. Co(II) and Co(III) coordination compounds display ligand-dependent geometry, spin state, and redox behaviour. Cobalt forms oxides, sulfides, halides, and organometallic complexes; cobalt-containing catalysts and coordination compounds are important in synthesis and industrial chemistry.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Cobalt is used in high-temperature superalloys, hardmetals, wear-resistant materials, and permanent-magnet compositions such as samarium–cobalt. Its electrochemical role in some lithium-ion battery cathodes makes supply-chain chemistry and recycling important materials-science topics.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Cobalt compounds require compound-specific hazard assessment. Inhalation of cobalt-containing dusts and aerosols is a significant occupational concern; some cobalt compounds are classified as carcinogenic hazards. Radioactive ⁶⁰Co requires radiological controls and shielding appropriate to its penetrating gamma emissions.
