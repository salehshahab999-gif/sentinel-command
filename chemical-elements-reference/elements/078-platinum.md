# Element 78: Platinum

**Symbol:** Pt  
**Atomic number:** 78  
**Group / period / block:** 10 / 6 / d  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 195.084(9)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d⁹ 6s  
**Ground-state term:** ³D₃  
**First ionization energy:** 8.95883 eV (NIST ASD)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁹⁰Pt, ¹⁹²Pt, ¹⁹⁴Pt, ¹⁹⁵Pt, ¹⁹⁶Pt and ¹⁹⁸Pt  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; detailed isotope, catalysis and compound-specific toxicology reviews remain open.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Platinum is a group-10 transition metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d⁹6s and ground term ³D₃. Its chemistry is dominated by oxidation states +2 and +4, although other formal oxidation states occur in specialised complexes and materials. Relativistic effects are significant in platinum and contribute to its electronic structure, bonding and catalytic behaviour. Platinum coordination chemistry is central to organometallic catalysis and bioinorganic chemistry.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative amount fractions are: ¹⁹⁰Pt 0.00012(2), ¹⁹²Pt 0.00782(24), ¹⁹⁴Pt 0.32864(410), ¹⁹⁵Pt 0.33775(240), ¹⁹⁶Pt 0.25211(340) and ¹⁹⁸Pt 0.07356(130). The standard atomic weight is 195.084(9). ¹⁹⁵Pt has nuclear spin I = 1/2 and is important in platinum NMR and coordination-chemistry studies. ¹⁹⁰Pt is an extremely long-lived alpha emitter; the precise decay data and uncertainties should be drawn from ENSDF/NuDat. Isotope abundance and nuclear stability should not be conflated.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
At ambient conditions platinum has a face-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1768.2 °C, boiling point 3825 °C and density 21.5 g cm⁻³. Platinum is ductile, malleable and highly resistant to oxidation and corrosion in many environments, although aqua regia can dissolve it by stabilising soluble chloro-complexes. Physical properties of nanoparticulate platinum differ from bulk metal because of surface area, particle size, support interactions and surface adsorbates.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Platinum forms stable coordination compounds, commonly in +2 and +4 oxidation states. Pt(II) complexes often adopt square-planar geometry, while Pt(IV) complexes are commonly octahedral. Ligand substitution kinetics, trans influence and strong metal–ligand covalency make platinum a central system in coordination chemistry. Platinum catalyses hydrogenation, oxidation, dehydrogenation and electrochemical reactions; activity depends on facets, adsorbate coverage, support and reaction conditions.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Platinum is widely used in automotive catalytic converters, chemical-process catalysts, fuel-cell electrodes, thermocouples, optical and electrical components, and high-performance alloys. Platinum compounds such as cisplatin and related drugs are used in cancer chemotherapy; their biological activity and toxicity belong to the specific molecular complex, not elemental platinum metal. Recycling from catalytic converters, electronics and jewellery is important because platinum-group metals are scarce and supply is geographically concentrated.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Bulk platinum metal is comparatively inert. Some soluble platinum salts can be potent sensitizers and may cause allergic or occupational respiratory effects. Platinum-based chemotherapy compounds have cytotoxic and systemic adverse effects, which are dose- and drug-specific. Environmental assessments should distinguish metallic particles, soluble salts, catalyst residues and pharmaceutical residues.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Platinum was known and worked in pre-Columbian South America and was described to European science in the sixteenth century. The name derives from Spanish *platina*, meaning little silver. Platinum's chemical resistance and catalytic properties made it important in the development of industrial catalysis and modern organometallic chemistry.
