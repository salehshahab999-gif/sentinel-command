# Element 72: Hafnium

**Symbol:** Hf  
**Atomic number:** 72  
**Group / period / block:** 4 / 6 / d  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 178.486(6)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d² 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ³F₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.82507 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ¹⁷⁴Hf, ¹⁷⁶Hf, ¹⁷⁷Hf, ¹⁷⁸Hf, ¹⁷⁹Hf and ¹⁸⁰Hf  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific nuclear, neutron-capture and high-temperature material data require further evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Hafnium is a group-4 transition metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d²6s² and ground term ³F₂. Its chemistry is dominated by the +4 oxidation state, Hf⁴⁺, with a xenon-like closed-shell core after removal of the four outer electrons. The near-identical ionic radii and chemistry of Hf⁴⁺ and Zr⁴⁺ make hafnium and zirconium unusually difficult to separate. This similarity has major consequences for nuclear-grade zirconium production, where hafnium removal is important because hafnium absorbs thermal neutrons much more strongly.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative isotopic amount fractions are: ¹⁷⁴Hf 0.00161(2), ¹⁷⁶Hf 0.0524(14), ¹⁷⁷Hf 0.1858(9), ¹⁷⁸Hf 0.2728(6), ¹⁷⁹Hf 0.1363(3) and ¹⁸⁰Hf 0.3512(16). The standard atomic weight is 178.486(6). ¹⁷⁴Hf is an extremely long-lived alpha emitter, with a half-life of approximately 2.0(4)×10¹⁵ years. ¹⁷⁶Hf is the radiogenic daughter of ¹⁷⁶Lu and is central to Lu-Hf isotope geochronology. Evaluated isotope masses, branching data and uncertainty should be taken from CIAAW and current ENSDF/NuDat records.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At ambient conditions hafnium is a dense, corrosion-resistant, silvery metal with a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation lists a melting point of 2233 °C, boiling point of 4600 °C and density of 13.3 g cm⁻³. Hf remains mechanically useful at high temperatures and forms a protective oxide layer. These compact reference values should be supplemented in a specialist edition by temperature-dependent heat capacity, thermal conductivity, elastic constants, phase boundaries and high-temperature oxidation kinetics.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The +4 state dominates ordinary hafnium chemistry. HfO₂ is a refractory oxide with high dielectric permittivity and strong chemical stability. Hafnium halides and organohafnium compounds are also known. Hf⁴⁺ is a hard Lewis acid with high coordination numbers and a strong preference for oxygen and fluorine donors. The close chemical similarity to zirconium leads to co-occurrence in minerals such as zircon and requires specialised solvent-extraction or ion-exchange processes for separation.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Hafnium is used in nuclear control rods because of its high thermal-neutron absorption and good corrosion resistance. Hafnium-containing superalloys are used in high-temperature aerospace components. HfO₂ and hafnium silicates are important high-k gate dielectrics in advanced semiconductor devices, where interfacial chemistry, oxygen vacancies, phase stability and film thickness influence device performance. Hafnium carbide is an ultra-high-temperature ceramic; its properties must be distinguished from those of elemental hafnium.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Bulk hafnium metal is relatively corrosion-resistant, but fine powders and machining dust require appropriate controls. Hafnium compounds vary in solubility and hazard. In nuclear applications, neutron-activated hafnium can contain radioactive isotopes and requires radiological management. Mining and refining may involve zirconium-bearing ores, acids and solvent-extraction reagents.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Hafnium was discovered in 1923 by Dirk Coster and George de Hevesy in Copenhagen, following predictions based on atomic structure. The name derives from *Hafnia*, the Latin name for Copenhagen. Its identification confirmed the prediction that the element following lutetium would occur with zirconium rather than among the rare-earth fractions.
