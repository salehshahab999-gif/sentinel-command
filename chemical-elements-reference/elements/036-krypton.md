# Element 36: Krypton

**Symbol:** Kr  
**Atomic number:** 36  
**Group / period / block:** 18 / 4 / p  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 83.798(2)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 13.9996 eV (PDG 2025 review of NIST data)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ⁷⁸Kr, ⁸⁰Kr, ⁸²Kr, ⁸³Kr, ⁸⁴Kr and ⁸⁶Kr  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Gas  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific nuclear and high-pressure phase data remain open.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Krypton is a closed-shell noble gas with neutral ground configuration [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p⁶ and term ¹S₀. Its high first ionization energy and filled valence shell explain its low chemical reactivity under ordinary conditions. Krypton compounds can nevertheless be produced under specialised conditions; krypton difluoride (KrF₂) is an established example. Atomic spectroscopy is important in discharge lamps, excimer systems and precision metrology.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW representative amount fractions are ⁷⁸Kr 0.00355(3), ⁸⁰Kr 0.02286(10), ⁸²Kr 0.11593(31), ⁸³Kr 0.11500(19), ⁸⁴Kr 0.56987(15) and ⁸⁶Kr 0.17279(41). The standard atomic weight is 83.798(2). ⁸³Kr has nuclear spin I = 9/2 and is important in NMR and hyperpolarised-gas imaging research. ⁷⁸Kr and ⁸⁶Kr are extremely long-lived double-beta-decay nuclides; their exact nuclear status and half-lives should be taken from evaluated nuclear data.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar krypton is a colourless, odourless noble gas. The RSC compilation lists melting point −157.36 °C and boiling point −153.22 °C. Gas density is approximately 3.75 g L⁻¹ near standard reference conditions; the exact value depends on temperature and pressure. Solid krypton forms a close-packed molecularly nonbonded crystal at cryogenic temperatures, where dispersion forces dominate cohesion.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Krypton is chemically inert in ordinary environments but forms compounds under strongly oxidising or photochemical conditions. KrF₂ is the best-known binary compound and can act as a powerful fluorinating and oxidising agent. Oxidation states and compound stability should be discussed for the specific molecule and experimental conditions; a noble-gas classification does not imply absolute chemical inertness.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Krypton is used in specialised lighting, flash lamps, insulating window units, lasers and research systems. KrF excimer lasers have applications in photolithography and precision ultraviolet processing. Krypton-83 is used in nuclear magnetic resonance and hyperpolarised-gas imaging research. The optical output and efficiency of a krypton discharge source depend on pressure, current, gas purity and lamp geometry.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Krypton is non-toxic in the usual chemical sense but can displace oxygen in confined spaces and act as a simple asphyxiant. High-pressure cylinders require gas-handling precautions. Radioactive krypton isotopes, particularly fission products such as ⁸⁵Kr, require isotope-specific radiological and environmental monitoring.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Krypton was discovered in 1898 by William Ramsay and Morris Travers while fractionally distilling liquid air. Its name derives from Greek *kryptos*, meaning hidden, reflecting its low abundance and difficult detection.
