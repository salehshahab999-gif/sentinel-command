# Element 69: Thulium

**Symbol:** Tm  
**Atomic number:** 69  
**Group / period / block:** lanthanoid / 6 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 168.934 219(5)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹³ 6s²  
**Ground-state term:** ²F°₇/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 6.18431 eV (NIST Atomic Data Handbook)  
**Naturally occurring nuclide:** ¹⁶⁹Tm (monoisotopic)  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope-specific nuclear and laser-material evaluations remain open.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Thulium has neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹³6s² and ground term ²F°₇/₂. Its 4f shell is one electron short of a closed subshell. The +3 oxidation state dominates common chemistry, producing Tm³⁺ with 4f¹². Tm²⁺ is also known in selected halides and solid-state compounds, where its stability depends on ligand and lattice environment. The element's optical spectrum arises from 4f multiplets and is sensitive to host-lattice crystal fields.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear properties
Natural thulium is monoisotopic: ¹⁶⁹Tm is the sole naturally occurring stable isotope and has representative amount fraction 1. CIAAW gives a standard atomic weight of 168.934219(5). The isotope has nuclear spin I = 1/2. Other thulium isotopes are radioactive and are produced in nuclear reactions. ¹⁷⁰Tm is a beta-emitting radionuclide used in specialised radiation-source applications; its exact half-life, beta spectrum and accompanying emissions must be verified using the current ENSDF/NuDat evaluation. The naturally occurring ¹⁶⁹Tm abundance entry must not be confused with the isotope composition of an activated source.

## 3. Physical and thermophysical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar thulium is a solid, bright silvery metal with a hexagonal close-packed structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1545 °C, boiling point 1950 °C and density 9.32 g cm⁻³. It is one of the less abundant naturally occurring lanthanoids. Bulk metal properties should be reported with purity, temperature, pressure and phase specified; surface oxidation and impurities can influence measurements.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The dominant oxidation state is +3. Tm₂O₃ and trivalent halides are representative compounds. Tm(II) chemistry occurs in selected reducing environments and solid-state materials. Tm³⁺ generally forms high-coordinate complexes with oxygen-donor ligands; aqueous speciation depends on pH and complexing agents. As with other lanthanoids, the small differences in ionic radii across the series are important in separation chemistry.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Tm-doped crystals and fibres are used in lasers, notably near 2 μm, where water absorption is useful for selected medical and industrial applications. Tm:YAG and thulium-doped fibre systems require host-specific optical and thermal characterisation. Reactor-produced ¹⁷⁰Tm has been used in compact beta-emitting sources and portable radiographic devices; this is an isotope-specific radiological application, not a property of natural thulium metal. Certain thulium compounds also have specialised roles in phosphors and ceramic materials.

## 6. Safety and radiological considerations
Natural thulium metal and compounds require ordinary rare-earth chemical hygiene, with attention to dust and reactive powders. Activated ¹⁷⁰Tm sources require radiological controls, activity measurement, containment and isotope-specific dosimetry. Radiation-source applications must distinguish beta emission from bremsstrahlung generated during shielding and material interactions.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Thulium was identified by Per Teodor Cleve in 1879 and named after *Thule*, a classical name for a far-northern region. Its late discovery reflects the difficulty of isolating the least abundant lanthanoids from chemically similar mixtures.
