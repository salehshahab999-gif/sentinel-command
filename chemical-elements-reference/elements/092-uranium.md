# Element 92: Uranium

**Symbol:** U  
**Atomic number:** 92  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 238.028 91(3)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f³ 6d 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ⁵L₆  
**First ionization energy:** 6.1941 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Naturally occurring isotopes:** ²³⁴U, ²³⁵U and ²³⁸U  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; isotope, geochemistry, materials and nuclear-fuel-cycle data require specialist evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Uranium is an actinoid with neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f³6d7s² and ground term ⁵L₆. Its 5f, 6d and 7s electrons can participate in bonding, giving uranium a rich range of oxidation states, commonly +3, +4, +5 and +6. Uranium(VI) forms the uranyl ion, UO₂²⁺, in many aqueous and solid compounds. The actinoid 5f orbitals are more spatially extended than lanthanoid 4f orbitals and contribute to covalency, complex formation and redox chemistry.

## 2. Isotopic composition and nuclear properties
CIAAW 2024 representative amount fractions are ²³⁴U 0.000054(5), ²³⁵U 0.007204(6) and ²³⁸U 0.992742(10). The standard atomic weight is 238.02891(3). Approximate half-lives are ²³⁴U 2.455×10⁵ years, ²³⁵U 7.04×10⁸ years and ²³⁸U 4.468×10⁹ years. These nuclides are alpha emitters and belong to distinct decay chains. Isotopic composition varies in some natural ores and can be modified in commercial materials; sample provenance and isotope-ratio measurement are important for geochemical and safeguards interpretation.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar uranium is a dense, silvery radioactive metal. Alpha-uranium has an orthorhombic crystal structure at room temperature and undergoes temperature-dependent transitions to beta and gamma allotropes. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1135 °C, boiling point 4131 °C and density 19.1 g cm⁻³. The allotropic transformations produce significant changes in density, ductility and mechanical behaviour. Corrosion, oxidation and hydride formation depend on surface condition and environment.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Uranium chemistry is dominated by +4 and +6 under many environmental conditions, with +3 and +5 also represented. UO₂ is a major uranium dioxide phase, while U₃O₈ and UO₃ are important oxides. In aqueous environments, uranyl carbonate and other complexes can strongly influence uranium mobility. Redox potential, pH, carbonate, phosphate, calcium and organic ligands are critical controls on speciation and transport. Total uranium concentration alone is insufficient to predict mobility or bioavailability.

## 5. Materials science and energy relevance
Uranium is used as a nuclear fuel in controlled reactor systems and as a reference material in nuclear science and geochemistry. Uranium dioxide is widely used as a ceramic fuel form, while uranium metal and other compounds have specialised applications. The performance of fuel materials depends on composition, irradiation history, burn-up, temperature, defect chemistry and radiation damage. This monograph is a general scientific reference and does not provide operational fuel fabrication or enrichment procedures.

## 6. Radiological and chemical safety
Uranium presents both chemical heavy-metal toxicity and radiological hazards. Internal exposure by inhalation or ingestion can affect kidneys and deliver radiation to tissues; chemical toxicity may be significant even when radiological activity is relatively low. Risk depends on isotope composition, particle size, solubility, route and duration of exposure. Uranium-bearing dust, contaminated liquids and process wastes require regulated controls and monitoring.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Uranium was discovered in 1789 by Martin Heinrich Klaproth in the mineral pitchblende and named after the planet Uranus. Its radioactivity was identified in 1896 by Henri Becquerel, leading to the development of nuclear physics and radiochemistry.
