# Element 32: Germanium

**Symbol:** Ge  
**Atomic number:** 32  
**Group:** 14  
**Period:** 4  
**Block:** p-block  
**Classification:** Post-transition metal / metalloid classification depends on convention  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** 72.630(8)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p²  
**Physical state at approximately 298 K and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Germanium is a period-4 p-block element. Its valence-shell configuration is represented by [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p². The element's bonding and chemistry depend on oxidation state, coordination environment, allotrope, and phase; a single periodic classification does not capture all of these behaviours.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Naturally occurring germanium has five stable isotopes: ⁷⁰Ge, ⁷²Ge, ⁷³Ge, ⁷⁴Ge, and ⁷⁶Ge. CIAAW gives representative amount fractions 0.2052(19), 0.2745(15), 0.0776(8), 0.3652(12), and 0.0775(12), respectively. Its standard atomic weight is 72.630(8).

**Evidence status:** Natural isotope masses and representative amount fractions are attributed to CIAAW. A complete evaluated nuclear chart, decay modes, branching ratios, and half-life uncertainties have not yet been compiled for this element.

## 3. Physical properties
At approximately 298 K and 1 bar, germanium is a brittle, grey-white crystalline solid with the diamond-cubic structure. RSC lists a melting point of 1211.4 K, boiling point of 3106 K, and density of 5.3234 g cm⁻³. Electrical behaviour is strongly temperature- and impurity-dependent because germanium is a semiconductor.

**Evidence status:** Reference values are drawn from the RSC periodic-table fact box. These are secondary reference values and are not yet a critical compilation of primary measurements. Phase, allotrope, pressure, purity, and uncertainty must be specified before final release.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Germanium commonly exhibits +4 and +2 oxidation states. The +4 state is prominent in GeO₂ and tetrahedral germanium compounds; organogermanium chemistry includes compounds with Ge–C bonds. Germanium dioxide has polymorphic and coordination-dependent structural chemistry, so phase labels should accompany physical data.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Germanium is used in infrared optics, fibre-optic systems, semiconductor devices, radiation detectors, and selected photovoltaic technologies. High-purity single crystals and controlled doping are essential for electronic applications. Germanium-containing optical materials exploit its high refractive index and infrared transparency.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Toxicity depends strongly on chemical form. Some inorganic germanium compounds and organogermanium substances have different toxicological profiles; chronic ingestion of certain germanium supplements has been associated with serious renal injury. Avoid blanket claims that all germanium compounds are harmless.
