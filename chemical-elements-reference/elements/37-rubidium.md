# Element 37: Rubidium

**Symbol:** Rb  
**Atomic number:** 37  
**Group:** 1  
**Period:** 5  
**Block:** s-block  
**Classification:** Alkali metal  
**Standard atomic weight (CIAAW):** 85.4678(3)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 5s¹  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid

## 1. Atomic and periodic context
Rubidium is a period-5 s-block element. Its ground-state electron configuration is [Kr] 5s¹. The periodic classification is useful for orientation, but chemical properties must be interpreted in terms of electronic structure, phase, oxidation state, and reaction conditions.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear context
Natural rubidium consists of stable ⁸⁵Rb (representative amount fraction 0.7217(2)) and radioactive ⁸⁷Rb (0.2783(2)). CIAAW gives ⁸⁷Rb a beta-minus half-life of 48.8(5) billion years. Its decay produces stable ⁸⁷Sr. This long-lived natural radioactivity is central to Rb–Sr geochronology and does not measurably change the standard atomic weight at its stated precision over ordinary geological sample histories.

## 3. Physical properties
At 20 °C and 1 bar rubidium is a soft, silvery alkali metal and solid, but it melts near room temperature. RSC reference values are melting point 312.45 K, boiling point 961 K, and density 1.53 g cm⁻³. The metal is highly reactive, and its physical measurements require rigorous exclusion of air and moisture.

## 4. Chemical behaviour and bonding
Rubidium is strongly electropositive and forms Rb⁺ in ordinary compounds. It reacts vigorously with water and oxygen, producing hydroxide and oxide/peroxide/superoxide products depending on conditions. Rubidium salts are generally ionic and often water-soluble. The metal must be stored under suitable inert or hydrocarbon media.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Rubidium has specialized uses in atomic-frequency standards, research on laser cooling and ultracold atoms, photocathodes, vacuum technology, and selected specialty glasses. The ⁸⁷Rb hyperfine transition is widely used in atomic clocks and quantum-technology experiments. Rb–Sr isotope systematics are a major tool in geochronology and isotope geochemistry.

## 6. Toxicology and handling
Rubidium metal reacts violently with water and can ignite in air; contact may cause severe thermal and chemical burns. Fire response and storage require alkali-metal-specific procedures. Natural ⁸⁷Rb is weakly radioactive, but the dominant practical laboratory hazard of bulk metal is its extreme chemical reactivity.
