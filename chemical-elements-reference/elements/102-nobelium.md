# Element 102: Nobelium

**Symbol:** No  
**Atomic number:** 102  
**Group / period / block:** actinoid / 7 / f  
**Standard atomic weight:** none; representative mass number [259]  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 7s²  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 6.6262 eV (PDG 2024 review of NIST data)  
**Representative isotope:** ²⁵⁹No  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; direct physical properties remain unavailable and atomic/nuclear values are based on few-atom experiments and evaluated compilations.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Nobelium is the final actinoid by atomic-number convention and has neutral ground configuration [Rn]5f¹⁴7s² with ground term ¹S₀. The closed 5f shell stabilises the +2 oxidation state, making No(II) unusually important compared with earlier actinoids. The +3 state is also known in selected experiments. This chemistry is a key test of actinoid periodic trends and the completion of the 5f shell.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopes
Nobelium has no stable isotopes and no standard atomic weight. [259] is a representative mass number. ²⁵⁹No has a half-life of about 58 minutes; other isotopes have shorter or longer but still finite half-lives. Nuclear reaction products are detected through characteristic decay chains and recoil-separator experiments. Each isotope's half-life, decay mode and daughter sequence must be obtained from current ENSDF/NuDat and NUBASE evaluations.

## 3. Physical properties and measurement limits
No macroscopic sample of nobelium exists. Density, melting point, boiling point, crystal structure and bulk transport properties are not experimentally established. Some periodic-table websites provide predicted values, but such estimates vary by model and should not be included as certified reference constants. The defensible status for bulk physical properties is “unknown or theoretical.”

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Nobelium is unusual among actinoids because the +2 oxidation state is strongly stabilised by its closed 5f¹⁴ configuration. Trace-scale experiments have demonstrated No²⁺ chemistry and have been used to compare its complexation and extraction behaviour with neighbouring divalent lanthanoids and actinoids. No(III) can also occur, depending on experimental conditions. Chemical data are based on very small atom counts and require careful error and timescale treatment.

## 5. Research relevance
Nobelium has no commercial or technological uses. Its importance is in nuclear structure, the chemistry of the heaviest elements, and testing the limits of periodic trends. Experiments require accelerator facilities, fast chemical separation and single-atom detection.

## 6. Radiological safety
Nobelium is radioactive and is studied only in highly specialised nuclear-research facilities. Isotope-specific decay products and alpha or other radiation emissions require licensed controls and contamination prevention. Because only trace quantities are available, the practical hazard is managed through facility-level radiological controls rather than bulk chemical handling.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Nobelium was reported by scientists at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna in 1966, following earlier disputed claims. Subsequent international evaluation recognised the discovery work and the name Nobelium, in honour of Alfred Nobel, was adopted by IUPAC.
