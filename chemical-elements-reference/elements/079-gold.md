# Element 79: Gold

**Symbol:** Au  
**Atomic number:** 79  
**Group / period / block:** 11 / 6 / d  
**CIAAW standard atomic weight:** 196.966 570(4)  
**Neutral ground-state configuration:** [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s  
**Ground-state term:** ²S₁/₂  
**First ionization energy:** 9.2256 eV (NIST/PDG compilation)  
**Naturally occurring nuclide:** ¹⁹⁷Au (monoisotopic)  
**Evidence status:** structured research draft; surface, catalytic and nanomaterial data need compound- and protocol-specific evaluation.

## 1. Atomic and electronic structure
Gold is a group-11 transition metal with neutral ground configuration [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s and ground term ²S₁/₂. Relativistic effects are particularly important in gold and contribute to its electronic structure, colour, bonding and redox behaviour. Common oxidation states are +1 and +3, with +2 and higher oxidation states appearing in specialised complexes and clusters. Gold(I) often forms linear two-coordinate complexes, while Au(III) commonly forms square-planar complexes.

## 2. Isotopic and nuclear properties
Natural gold is monoisotopic: ¹⁹⁷Au has representative amount fraction 1 and is stable. CIAAW gives the standard atomic weight 196.966570(4), determined solely by the mass of ¹⁹⁷Au. The nucleus has spin I = 3/2. Radioactive gold isotopes are produced in reactors, accelerators and nuclear reactions; decay schemes and emissions are isotope-specific and must be obtained from current ENSDF/NuDat records.

## 3. Physical and condensed-matter properties
At ambient pressure gold adopts a face-centred cubic structure. The RSC compilation lists melting point 1064.18 °C, boiling point 2836 °C and density 19.3 g cm⁻³. Gold is malleable and ductile and has high electrical and thermal conductivity. Its characteristic yellow colour is influenced by relativistic electronic effects. Surface cleanliness, grain boundaries, alloy composition and nanostructuring influence measured mechanical, optical and electrical properties.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
Bulk gold is chemically resistant to oxidation and many acids, but it dissolves in aqua regia and in selected ligand systems such as cyanide under oxidising conditions. Gold forms a broad range of coordination complexes and organometallic compounds. Au(I) and Au(III) chemistry is central to homogeneous catalysis, medicinal inorganic chemistry and surface functionalisation. Gold nanoparticles can display catalytic activity unlike that of bulk metal because of their high surface-to-volume ratio and support interactions.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Gold is used in jewellery, coinage, investment bullion, electrical contacts and corrosion-resistant connectors. Thin films and bonding wires are used in electronics. Gold nanoparticles and supported gold catalysts are used in selected oxidation and fine-chemical reactions. Gold compounds have specialised biomedical and research uses, but the properties and safety of a gold complex cannot be inferred from the inertness of bulk metallic gold. Alloy fineness, impurity profile and microstructure are important in commercial materials.

## 6. Safety and environmental considerations
Bulk metallic gold has low chemical reactivity, but soluble gold salts and organogold compounds can have distinct biological and environmental effects. Cyanide-based extraction and refining processes involve severe hazards that are separate from the toxicity of gold metal itself. Mining impacts, mercury amalgamation in artisanal gold extraction and tailings management are significant environmental concerns.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Gold has been known since prehistory and is among the earliest metals worked by humans. The symbol Au derives from Latin *aurum*. Its resistance to corrosion, rarity and visible colour made it important in ornament, trade, coinage and political symbolism across many civilisations.
