# Element 48: Cadmium

**Symbol:** Cd  
**Atomic number:** 48  
**Group / period / block:** 12 / 5 / d  
**Standard atomic weight:** 112.414(4)  
**Ground-state electron configuration:** [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s²  
**Ground-state term:** ¹S₀  
**First ionization energy:** 8.9938 eV  
**State at 20 °C and 1 bar:** Solid  
**Evidence status:** Initial research draft; see evidence qualifications below.

## 1. Atomic and quantum properties
Cadmium has a filled 4d subshell and a 5s² valence configuration. The closed-shell ground state is ¹S₀. The configuration, term, and first ionization energy are listed in the NIST periodic-table compilation; a publication-grade spectral appendix must verify them against the current NIST ASD and capture uncertainties, level classifications, and transition data.

## 2. Nuclear properties and isotopic composition
CIAAW gives standard atomic weight 112.414(4). Natural cadmium contains multiple stable isotopes with representative amount fractions: ¹⁰⁶Cd 0.01245(22), ¹⁰⁸Cd 0.00888(11), ¹¹⁰Cd 0.12470(61), ¹¹¹Cd 0.12795(12), ¹¹²Cd 0.24109(7), ¹¹³Cd 0.12227(7), ¹¹⁴Cd 0.28754(81), and ¹¹⁶Cd 0.07512(54). ¹¹³Cd is extremely long-lived and is conventionally included among naturally occurring isotopes; its decay data must be represented using the evaluated nuclear dataset rather than calling it strictly stable. Isotope-specific spins, metastable states, decay modes, and half-lives require ENSDF/NNDC verification.

## 3. Physical and thermodynamic properties
Cadmium is a solid metal at 20 °C and 1 bar and commonly adopts a hexagonal close-packed structure at ambient conditions. PubChem's periodic-table compilation lists a melting point of 594.22 K. That value is a compiled reference, not a full thermodynamic evaluation. Density, vapour pressure, heat capacity, thermal expansion, phase boundaries, and uncertainties should be added only with temperature and pressure specified and with traceable evaluated sources.

## 4. Chemical behaviour
The characteristic oxidation state is +2, with Cd(II) compounds generally displaying substantial ionic character. Cd²⁺ forms complexes with sulfur-, nitrogen-, and oxygen-donor ligands; its coordination chemistry is strongly influenced by ligand identity and solution conditions. Cadmium sulfide and cadmium selenide are technologically important semiconductors, while cadmium oxide, halides, and salts have distinct solubility and speciation behaviour. Cadmium is not a biologically required element in humans.

## 5. Materials science and applications
Historically important uses include nickel-cadmium batteries, corrosion-protective plating, pigments, low-melting alloys, control rods, and semiconductor materials such as CdTe and CdSe. Modern use is constrained by toxicity and regulation. CdTe photovoltaics depend on defect chemistry, grain boundaries, interfaces, and controlled stoichiometry; a serious materials chapter should include phase equilibria and device-layer interfaces rather than treating CdTe as a simple binary compound.

## 6. Toxicology and safety
Cadmium and many of its compounds are highly toxic. Chronic exposure can damage kidneys and bone; inhalation of cadmium-containing fumes or dust is particularly hazardous and can cause severe pulmonary injury. Cadmium is classified as a human carcinogen by major health authorities. Risk depends on chemical species, route, dose, duration, and workplace controls. Battery recycling and metallurgical operations require stringent exposure and waste management.

## 7. Discovery and nomenclature
Cadmium was identified in 1817 by Friedrich Stromeyer during investigation of zinc carbonate impurities. The name is associated with *cadmia*, a historical term for calamine/zinc ore. The element's discovery is closely tied to analytical chemistry and the purification of zinc compounds.
