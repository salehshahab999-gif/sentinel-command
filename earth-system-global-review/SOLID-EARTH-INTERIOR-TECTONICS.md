# Solid Earth, Deep Interior and Plate Tectonics
## Global geophysical assessment: 3 November 2026

## 1. What is measured and what is inferred

The Earth's interior cannot be directly photographed. Its structure is inferred from seismic-wave travel times, wave reflections and conversions, gravity, geomagnetic observations, Earth's rotation, laboratory mineral physics and numerical models.

Satellites contribute important information about gravity and the magnetic field, but they do not provide direct images of the core or mantle. Any claim that a satellite has visually detected a change inside the core is incorrect.

## 2. Reference structure of the Earth

A commonly used one-dimensional reference is the Preliminary Reference Earth Model (PREM). Approximate depth ranges are:

| Layer | Approximate depth | Physical state / role |
|---|---:|---|
| Crust | Surface to roughly 5–70 km, highly variable | Solid silicate rock; oceanic crust is generally thinner than continental crust |
| Lithosphere | Crust plus mechanically strong uppermost mantle | Rigid tectonic plates |
| Asthenosphere | Broadly around 80–220 km in a simplified reference description | Mostly solid rock that can deform over geological time |
| Upper mantle | To about 660 km | Solid silicate mantle |
| Mantle transition zone | About 410–660 km | Mineral phase transitions and changing seismic properties |
| Lower mantle | About 660–2,891 km | Solid, slowly convecting rock |
| Core–mantle boundary region | Around 2,891 km | Major thermal, chemical and seismic boundary |
| Outer core | About 2,891–5,150 km | Liquid iron-rich alloy; source of the main geomagnetic field through geodynamo action |
| Inner core | About 5,150–6,371 km | Solid iron-rich alloy |

The crust and lithosphere are not the same thing. The lithosphere includes the uppermost mantle and forms the tectonic plates.

Reference:
- [PREM and standard Earth models overview](https://doi.org/10.1036/1097-8542.209200)

## 3. The mantle and core

The outer core is liquid because it does not transmit ordinary shear S-waves in the same way as the solid mantle. The inner core is solid under the very high pressure at Earth's centre. The core–mantle boundary is a region of strong thermal and seismic contrast.

Research about the inner core's differential rotation and the deep mantle is active. A USGS-indexed 2026 paper in Communications Earth & Environment cautions that some claimed decade-scale changes in the lowermost mantle and outer core may be explained by imperfect repeatability of earthquake sources or station-related artifacts. This is a warning about evidence quality, not proof that the deep Earth is perfectly static.

Source:
- [USGS: Evaluation of seismological evidence for decadal changes in the lowermost mantle and outer core, 21 September 2026](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/evaluation-seismological-evidence-decadal-changes-earths-lowermost-mantle-and-outer)

### What would count as strong evidence of a deep-interior change?

A robust claim should include:
1. multiple independent earthquake sources or repeaters;
2. stable and well-characterized receiving stations;
3. consistent phase identification and waveform processing;
4. tests for source-location and source-mechanism changes;
5. independent observations from other seismic paths;
6. a physical model explaining the measured change.

Without these controls, small time shifts in waveforms may be misattributed to the core or mantle.

## 4. Geomagnetic field and satellite monitoring

The geomagnetic field is generated mainly by motion of electrically conducting liquid metal in the outer core. It varies in space and time. ESA's Swarm constellation measures the magnetic field from low Earth orbit and helps separate the main field, crustal field, ionospheric and magnetospheric contributions.

The South Atlantic Anomaly is a broad region of relatively weak geomagnetic field. Its importance is practical as well as scientific: satellites passing through it can experience increased exposure to energetic charged particles.

Important distinctions:
- A regional weakening does not prove an imminent magnetic reversal.
- Magnetic north drift does not mean the geographic pole is moving by the same amount.
- Short-term field variation should not be used as an explanation for recent global atmospheric warming.
- Core-flow models are inversions and physical interpretations, not direct observations of liquid metal.

Sources:

- [NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information - Geomagnetism](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/geomagnetic-data)

## 5. Plate tectonics: opening, collision and lateral motion

Continents do not all move apart. They are carried by tectonic plates, which interact in three principal ways:

- **Divergent boundaries:** plates move apart and new crust may form.
- **Convergent boundaries:** plates approach; subduction or continental collision may occur.
- **Transform boundaries:** plates slide horizontally past one another.

Examples:
- The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a divergent plate boundary.
- The East African Rift is an active continental rift system.
- The Himalaya records convergence between India and Eurasia.
- The Andes are strongly influenced by subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath South America.
- The Pacific margins include multiple subduction and transform systems.

Typical plate speeds range from millimetres to several centimetres per year. A single global speed is not scientifically meaningful.

## 6. How to measure present-day crustal motion

The strongest modern methods include:

**GNSS/GPS:** Continuous or campaign observations estimate three-dimensional station velocity relative to a defined reference frame.

**InSAR:** Repeated radar observations measure ground displacement along the satellite line of sight. It is especially useful for volcanic inflation, subsidence, earthquake deformation and landslides.

**Seismic moment tensors:** Help identify faulting style and the geometry of earthquake rupture.

**Paleomagnetism:** Records the orientation and movement of crustal blocks over geological time.

**Seafloor magnetic anomalies:** Help estimate ocean-floor spreading history and reconstruct plate motion.

For a valid velocity comparison, the researcher must record the reference frame, observation period, station quality, uncertainty and whether the velocity is absolute or relative to another plate.

## 7. Rift systems and continental extension

The East African Rift is a major example of continental extension. GNSS and geological studies show that extension rates vary along its branches and between crustal blocks. Rates are generally on the order of millimetres per year, not kilometres per year.

The Red Sea and Gulf of Aden are part of a broader divergent system between Africa and Arabia. Their evolution is linked to the East African Rift, but different segments have different ages, crustal structures and spreading rates.

The existence of extension does not mean an entire continent will split abruptly. Continental rifting, ocean-basin formation and plate reorganization occur over geological timescales, commonly millions of years.

## 8. Geochemistry and subsurface rock records

The Earth's crust and mantle are studied through rock samples, drilling, geochemical analyses and geophysical imaging.

Useful databases:
- [GEOROC](https://georoc.eu/) for geochemical and isotopic analyses of igneous and metamorphic rocks.
- [Macrostrat](https://www.macrostrat.org/map) for geological units, stratigraphy and mapped rock records.
- [British Geological Survey GeoIndex](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/map-viewers/geoindex-onshore/) for boreholes and subsurface geological records in Britain.
- [PANGAEA](https://www.pangaea.de/) for earth and environmental science datasets.
- [EarthChem](https://www.earthchem.org/) for geochemical data and sample discovery.

A database containing many samples does not imply uniform global coverage. Sample density is strongly biased toward accessible, studied and funded regions.

## 9. Global interpretation

Current plate motion, geomagnetic variation and deep-Earth dynamics are normal parts of a living planet. Evidence supports ongoing deformation and evolution, but it does not show that all tectonic plates are accelerating together or that the core is undergoing a sudden global event.

The correct approach is to assess each process using a suitable physical observable:
- plate motion with GNSS and geological reconstruction;
- crustal deformation with GNSS and InSAR;
- mantle and core with seismic tomography and mineral physics;
- magnetic-field change with Swarm and ground geomagnetic observatories;
- rock composition with laboratory geochemistry and curated sample archives.

Any future claim of rapid global solid-Earth change should be required to pass independent replication across methods and research groups.
