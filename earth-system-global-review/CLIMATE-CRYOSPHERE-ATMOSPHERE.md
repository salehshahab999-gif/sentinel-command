# Climate, Cryosphere and Atmosphere
## Global evidence snapshot: 3 November 2026

## 1. Global temperature

The World Meteorological Organization's State of the Global Climate 2025 report, published on 23 March 2026, assesses 2025 at approximately **1.43 ± 0.13 °C above the 1850–1900 average**. It ranks 2025 as the second or third warmest year, depending on the dataset. WMO also identifies 2015–2025 as the warmest eleven-year sequence in the observational record.

Berkeley Earth's September 2026 update places September at approximately **1.65 ± 0.11 °C above 1850–1900**, the second-warmest September in its record. It estimates a high probability that 2026 will become the warmest calendar year, but that is a conditional forecast based on the developing climate pattern and must not be labelled as a final annual observation.

NOAA NCEI reports that January–September 2026 was the second-warmest year-to-date period in its record, behind 2024. Japan Meteorological Agency's 2025 monitoring page also reports the 2025 global mean anomaly, but relative to 1991–2020 rather than 1850–1900.

**Interpretation:** Different agencies agree on the direction of the long-term trend. Their anomaly values should not be compared without recording the baseline, spatial coverage, aggregation method and dataset version.

## 2. Arctic and Antarctic sea ice

Sea ice is floating ocean ice. Its melting has a different direct sea-level effect from the melting of land-based ice sheets and glaciers. Extent, area, concentration, thickness and volume are distinct variables.

### Arctic 2026

NASA and NSIDC place the 2026 annual minimum at **4.60 million km² on 12 September**, tied for the tenth-lowest minimum in the satellite record. The last twenty years contain the twenty lowest annual minima in the continuous satellite record.

Other observing systems give different values:
- Japan's NIPR Arctic Sea-Ice Information Center reports **4.50 million km² on 11 September**, also tenth-lowest, and a winter maximum of **13.76 million km²**, a new low in its product record. NIPR separately reports the Antarctic annual maximum at **17.71 million km² on 11 September**, the third-lowest in its satellite record. Its September monthly mean was about **17.39 million km²**, also third-lowest. NIPR's sea-ice extent in this report is calculated using a five-day average. This is a distinct NIPR product and should not be substituted for the NASA/NSIDC or OSI SAF series.
- The U.S. National Ice Center reports a **three-day MASIE average of 4.77 million km²** centred on 14 September.
- The UK Met Office's OSI SAF-based briefing reports **5.06 million km²** and ranks it fourteenth-lowest.

These are not interchangeable values. They use different products, algorithms, temporal averaging and ice-edge definitions. See [Cross-country source reconciliation](CROSS-COUNTRY-SOURCE-RECONCILIATION.md).

### Antarctic 2026

NASA/NSIDC report a likely annual maximum of **17.59 million km² on 14 September**, the third-lowest in their satellite record. EUMETSAT OSI SAF's daily product reports **18.191 million km² on 10 September**, with only two years having a lower maximum in that product's 48-year record.

The products do not return the same area or ranking. Both indicate an exceptionally low annual maximum. The product name and method must travel with every number.

## 3. Land ice: Greenland, Antarctica and mountain glaciers

NASA's GRACE and GRACE Follow-On gravity measurements indicate average mass losses over 2002–2025 of approximately:
- **Greenland: 264 gigatonnes per year**
- **Antarctica: 135 gigatonnes per year**

These are multi-year averages over the stated period, not the mass lost in 2025 alone. Regional gains, particularly from snowfall in parts of East Antarctica, can coexist with larger net losses elsewhere.

The World Glacier Monitoring Service estimates that glaciers outside the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets lost **408 ± 132 Gt** during hydrological year 2025, corresponding to approximately **1.1 ± 0.4 mm** of global mean sea-level rise. The largest regional mass-loss contributions came from High Mountain Asia, Alaska and the Russian Arctic; regional area-averaged losses were particularly strong in Western Canada and the United States, Iceland and Central Europe.

## 4. Greenhouse-gas concentrations

WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22, released on 27 October 2026, reports globally averaged surface concentrations for 2025:

| Gas | 2025 global mean | Increase above pre-industrial |
|---|---:|---:|
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | 426.8 ± 0.2 ppm | 53% |
| Methane (CH₄) | 1,948 ± 2 ppb | 167% |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O) | 339.1 ± 0.1 ppb | 26% |

These are atmospheric concentration measurements based on the WMO Global Atmosphere Watch in-situ network. They are not annual emissions totals.

CO₂'s 2024–2025 concentration increase was lower than the unusually high increase in 2023–2024, but slightly higher than the mean annual growth rate over 2016–2025. Methane's annual increase was lower than both the preceding year's increase and its decade average. N₂O continued to increase.

## 5. Fossil fuels and greenhouse-gas emissions

Two widely used 2026 assessments estimate 2025 emissions using different boundaries:

| Source | 2025 estimate | Change from 2024 | Scope |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Global Carbon Budget 2025 | 38.1 GtCO₂ | +1.0% | Fossil CO₂ including cement-related processes |
| IEA Global Energy Review 2026 | 38.4 GtCO₂ | +0.4% | Energy-related CO₂ |

Do not average these figures or call them contradictory. The accounting scopes differ. Global Carbon Budget estimates that coal, oil and gas emissions all increased in 2025. IEA estimates that energy-related CO₂ emissions increased by 145 Mt and reached a new high.

The European Commission's EDGAR 2026 report provides a complementary country-by-country greenhouse-gas inventory through 2025. It identifies China, the United States, India, the EU27, Russia and Indonesia as the six largest emitting economies in its accounting. Together, these account for about 62% of global greenhouse-gas emissions in that dataset. EDGAR reports different year-on-year movements among these economies; the data should be read with its sector and land-use boundaries.

## 6. Ocean heat and planetary energy imbalance

WMO reports that ocean heat content reached a record high in 2025 in the 66-year observational record. It also states that the Earth’s energy imbalance reached the highest level in its 65-year record. WMO estimates that the ocean has absorbed, on average, energy equivalent to about eighteen times annual human energy use per year over the past two decades.

A 2026 review in Nature Reviews Earth & Environment estimates that full-depth global ocean heat content increased by **24 ± 6 zettajoules** from 2024 to 2025 and by **481 ± 48 zettajoules** since 1960. A separate NCEI-derived upper-2,000-m dataset reports a 2025 anomaly of 32.5 × 10²² J relative to its long-term baseline; this is an anomaly, not the total heat content of the ocean.

Sources:
- [WMO State of the Global Climate 2025](https://wmo.int/publication-series/state-of-global-climate/state-of-global-climate-2025)
- [Nature Reviews: Ocean heat content in 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-026-00775-1)
- [Copernicus ocean heat content indicator](https://climate.copernicus.eu/climate-indicators/ocean-heat-content)

## 7. Global sea level

NASA's satellite-altimetry indicator reported **115.8 ± 4.0 mm** of global mean sea-level rise above the 1993 reference in October 2026.

Global mean sea level is affected by:
- mass added from melting glaciers and ice sheets;
- thermal expansion as seawater warms;
- changes in land-water storage;
- regional ocean circulation and gravitational effects.

A local coast can experience a different relative sea-level rate because the land itself may subside or rise. Subsidence caused by groundwater extraction, sediment compaction or tectonics must be separated from global ocean rise.

Source:
- [NASA Sea Level Earth Indicator](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/explore/earth-indicators/sea-leve/)

## 8. Antarctic ozone hole

CAMS reported that the 2026 Antarctic ozone-hole area reached approximately **25 million km² on 12 September**, around 5 million km² above the average for that point in the season. Its ozone-column minimum and ozone-mass-deficit indicators, however, remained near average at that time.

This distinction matters: ozone-hole area is one diagnostic, not a complete measurement of ozone loss. The size and depth of the hole vary with stratospheric temperature, polar-vortex strength, sunlight and chemical conditions.

NASA Ozone Watch provides daily satellite-derived maps and data for the 2026 season. Its threshold definition is based on total-column ozone below 220 Dobson Units south of 40°S. CAMS and NASA products should be cited with their own definitions and timestamps.

Sources:
- [CAMS 2026 ozone-hole update, 16 September](https://atmosphere.copernicus.eu/copernicus-ozone-hole-reaches-notable-threshold-early-again-area-increases-above-average-2026)
- [NASA Ozone Watch](https://ozonewatch.gsfc.nasa.gov/)

## 9. Regional variation and country-level checks

National annual summaries show why global averages must not be interpreted as uniform local change:

- India's IMD reports 2025 mean land temperature 0.28 °C above its 1991–2020 national average, the eighth warmest since 1901.
- Türkiye's State Meteorological Service reports a 2025 mean of 15.1 °C, 1.2 °C above its 1991–2020 national mean, and precipitation 27.6% below the national normal.
- Japan's JMA reports that 2025 was the third-warmest year in its national temperature record.
- WMO's Africa report states that Africa has warmed faster than the global average since 1991 and that some African coasts have experienced sea-level rise above the global average since 1999.
- Europe's 2025 State of the Climate report describes widespread above-average temperatures, record sea-surface temperatures and reduced snow/ice cover.

These national and regional values use different spatial domains and baselines. They are evidence of regional diversity, not directly comparable global anomalies.

## 10. Practical interpretation

A colder regional season, a local increase in snow cover or one year of a higher sea-ice extent does not reverse the multi-decadal climate signal. Conversely, a high monthly temperature does not by itself establish a new long-term climate trend. The strongest conclusions come from the combination of long-term temperature series, atmospheric composition, ocean heat content, mass balance and sea-level measurements.
