# Cross-Country Source Reconciliation
## Comparing independent national agencies and international datasets

**Assessment cut-off:** 3 November 2026

## 1. Why cross-country comparison is necessary

No single agency measures every part of the Earth system. International assessments often combine national meteorological offices, satellite programmes, research institutes, ocean observatories and peer-reviewed datasets.

The purpose of this page is not to select one country's data as universally superior. It is to identify:
- where independent sources agree;
- where numerical values differ;
- whether the difference arises from baseline, sensor, definition or time window;
- which source is best suited to each question;
- what can and cannot be concluded from the comparison.

### Reconciliation rule

WMO's +1.43 °C global anomaly uses 1850–1900. JMA's +0.48 °C global anomaly uses 1991–2020. These numbers describe the same general warming system but are not directly comparable because the reference periods differ. National anomalies also have different land/sea coverage and station networks.

| Institution / product | 2026 Arctic minimum | Rank stated by source | Method note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| NASA / NSIDC | 4.60 million km², 12 September | Tenth-lowest | Satellite sea-ice extent series |
| NIPR Japan / Arctic Sea-Ice Information Center | 4.50 million km², 11 September | Tenth-lowest | NIPR product and processing |

## 4. Sea-ice cross-check: Antarctic 2026

NASA/NSIDC reports a likely maximum of 17.59 million km² on 14 September, third-lowest in its satellite record. EUMETSAT OSI SAF reports a daily maximum of 18.191 million km² on 10 September and states that only two years in its 48-year record had a lower maximum.

Japan's NIPR product reports **17.71 million km² on 11 September**, also third-lowest in its record, using a five-day average. Its September monthly mean was about **17.39 million km²**, third-lowest in that product. The three annual-maximum values are not interchangeable: the sensors, algorithms, ice-edge definitions and averaging differ. The broad conclusion is consistent: the 2026 Antarctic annual maximum was exceptionally low.

| Institution / product | 2026 Antarctic annual maximum | Rank stated by source | Method note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| NASA / NSIDC | 17.59 million km², 14 September | Third-lowest | NASA/NSIDC satellite extent product |
| NIPR Japan | 17.71 million km², 11 September | Third-lowest | Five-day averaged extent in NIPR product |
| EUMETSAT OSI SAF | 18.191 million km², 10 September | Only two lower years in its 48-year record | OSI SAF daily product |

The NIPR September monthly mean was about 17.39 million km² and was also third-lowest in that product. Monthly mean and annual-maximum values answer different questions and must not be compared as if they were the same statistic.

## 5. Atmospheric composition versus emissions inventories

Three different measurement families are required:

| Source | Variable | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| WMO Global Atmosphere Watch | CO₂, CH₄, N₂O concentrations | Atmospheric concentrations from in-situ observation network |
| Global Carbon Budget | Fossil CO₂ emissions | Global carbon emissions inventory and carbon-cycle budget |
| IEA Global Energy Review | Energy-related CO₂ emissions | Emissions associated with energy use |
| European Commission EDGAR | Country and sector GHG emissions | Harmonized country/sector emissions inventory |

WMO's 426.8 ppm CO₂ is an atmospheric concentration. The GCP figure of 38.1 GtCO₂ and IEA figure of 38.4 GtCO₂ are annual emissions estimates. These values cannot be converted into each other by direct comparison.

GCP and IEA differ because their scopes and accounting treatments differ. Their close but non-identical estimates support a robust conclusion that 2025 fossil/energy-related emissions remained near record levels.

Sources:
- [WMO GHG Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22)
- [Global Carbon Budget 2025](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/3211/2026/)
- [IEA Global Energy Review 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026)
- [EDGAR 2026](https://edgar.jrc.ec.europa.eu/report_2026?vis=ghgtot)

## 6. National volcano and earthquake monitoring

National alert systems are designed for local hazards and use different scales. The following agencies provide useful country-level cross-checks:

| Country / region | Primary monitoring agency | Example or monitoring role |
|---|---|---|
| United States | USGS Volcano Hazards Program | Ahyi Seamount, Alaska and Hawaii volcano monitoring |
| Japan | JMA and NIED | Volcano alert levels, seismic and tsunami networks |
| Iceland | Icelandic Meteorological Office | Reykjanes deformation and seismic monitoring |
| New Zealand | GeoNet / GNS Science | Whakaari and Ruapehu updates |
| Chile | SERNAGEOMIN / OVDAS | Andes volcano monitoring and alerts |
| Italy | INGV | Etna, Stromboli and Campi Flegrei monitoring |
| Russia / Kamchatka | KVERT and IVS FEB RAS | Kamchatka ash, thermal and dome activity |
| Indonesia | PVMBG / MAGMA Indonesia | National volcano monitoring and alert reporting |
| Philippines | PHIVOLCS | Volcano, earthquake and tsunami monitoring |
| Türkiye | AFAD and MTA | Earthquake monitoring and national geological mapping |

The existence of different alert systems is not a reason to rank countries by the colour of an alert. Always quote the full agency, volcano, alert level definition, aviation colour and bulletin date.

## 7. Ocean and geology cross-check

GEBCO_2026 is the international global bathymetric grid. Its compilation depends on national hydrographic offices, research vessels, the Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 project and other data contributors.

Japan's NIPR and JAMSTEC contribute polar and oceanographic observations. NOAA Ocean Exploration contributes ship-based sonar and ROV imagery. European institutions such as GEOMAR and the IHO contribute marine geoscience, seabed mapping and workshop research. These sources complement rather than replace one another.

For deep-Earth questions, USGS and EarthScope provide seismic evidence and data infrastructure, GFZ provides major geophysical research, and ESA Swarm contributes magnetic-field observations. No one satellite or national agency directly images Earth's core.

## 8. Public observations and community data

Citizen reports can strengthen the event timeline and help identify unreported local impacts, but they are not equivalent to calibrated sensors.

Recommended sources:
- EMSC and LastQuake for public felt reports;
- USGS Did You Feel It for earthquake intensity observations;
- Seabed 2030 and IHO crowdsourced bathymetry for vessel-sourced depth soundings;
- national weather and volcano-agency public observation channels;
- professional meteorology forums for model discussion, clearly marked as community opinion.

Repeated copies of one social-media post count as one source lineage, not independent corroboration.

## 9. Overall cross-country conclusion

The international evidence is more consistent than a simple list of differing numbers may suggest. Most differences arise because agencies are reporting different variables, periods, reference baselines or processing methods. When these distinctions are preserved, global warming, increased atmospheric greenhouse-gas concentrations, increasing ocean heat, rising sea level and net land-ice loss are strongly supported by independent data systems.

For geophysical hazards, the source network confirms ongoing activity but does not support an evidence-based claim of a single global earthquake or volcanic acceleration. The principal uncertainty is observational coverage, especially beneath the ocean.
